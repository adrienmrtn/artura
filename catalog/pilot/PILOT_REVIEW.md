## Unique Forms of Continuity in Space

*Umberto Boccioni, 1913 · Museum of Modern Art, New York · futurism*

> A bronze figure with no arms and no face, built to show speed itself.

How would you make a statue of speed? Not of a runner, frozen at one instant like a photograph, but of the running: the push, the rush of air, the place the body was half a second ago. In 1913 a 30-year-old Italian named Umberto Boccioni gave himself exactly that problem.

Boccioni belonged to the Futurists, a group of young Italian artists and poets in love with everything fast and new: trains, engines, electric light, crowds. They found their country's museums suffocating. He was a painter first, and had been making sculpture for only about a year.

**Did you know?** Futurism was launched in 1909 on the front page of a Paris newspaper. Its manifesto, a public declaration of aims, claimed that a roaring racing car was more beautiful than the Victory of Samothrace, the ancient Greek winged statue in the Louvre.

His idea: a moving body does not stop at its skin. It drags air behind it, and our eye smears one position into the next. So he modeled both at once, the muscle and its wake, as a single solid. That is the “continuity in space” of the title. The figure stands just over a meter tall (about 44 inches).

**[Guided tour]** From this angle the figure walks toward the left. Go from head to feet.

- *The head*: No eyes, no mouth. The face is a sharp ridge crossed by another, somewhere between a helmet and a machine part. This is nobody in particular.
- *No arms*: The chest swells forward like a hull cutting water, and the shoulders simply end. Boccioni drops the arms so nothing interrupts the forward drive.
- *The trailing leg*: Behind the calf, bronze streams out in pointed lobes like flames or a flag in wind. It is the air the leg has just pushed through, made solid.
- *Two blocks*: Each foot is planted on its own small block instead of one shared base. The gap between them makes the stride feel longer, like a step across a ditch.

Now the funny part. A striding figure, no arms, drapery flying backward: that describes the Victory of Samothrace almost word for word. The Futurists wanted to burn the past down, and Boccioni's most famous work rhymes with the very statue their manifesto mocked.

**Key takeaway.** Boccioni's leap was to sculpt what a body does to the space around it. The shapes that look like armor or flames are motion, given a form you can walk around.

He modeled the figure in plaster, cheap and white, and that is all he ever saw. In 1915 Italy entered the First World War and Boccioni, who had cheered for it, enlisted. In August 1916 he was thrown from his horse during cavalry training and died the next day. He was 33.

**Did you know?** Every bronze version of this sculpture, including the one in this photo, was cast after the artist's death, the first in 1931. Boccioni had written against bronze and marble as tired “noble” materials, so the gleaming gold surface was not his choice.

He had made several other striding figures in plaster that same year. After his death they were broken up and lost. This is the only one that survived, and its original plaster ended up far from Italy, in a museum in São Paulo, Brazil.

**Did you know?** Millions of Europeans carry this sculpture around without knowing it. Italy chose it for the back of its 20-cent euro coin.

**Key takeaway.** Go back to the opening question. The answer weighs many kilos of metal and has stood still on a pedestal for over a century, and it still looks as if it is about to leave the room.

- Which ancient statue does the figure oddly resemble? → The Victory of Samothrace

## The Garden of Earthly Delights

*Hieronymus Bosch, 1500 · Prado, Madrid · early_netherlandish*

> A man made of eggshell and tree trunks looks back at you from Hell. Nobody knows why.

A man stands in the dark with his feet in two small boats. His legs are hollow tree trunks. His body is a cracked eggshell with a tavern inside, and on his head sits a disc where demons parade around a pink bagpipe. He turns his pale face over his shoulder, almost amused.

**[Find it]** This creature hides somewhere in the painting below. Can you spot him?

- *The Tree-Man*: People call him the Tree-Man. Some historians suspect the face is Bosch's own, though nothing proves it. Remember that look over the shoulder: we will come back to it.

Now step back. The Tree-Man is one creature among hundreds in a triptych, a painting made of three hinged panels. Open, it measures about 2.2 by 3.9 meters (roughly 7 by 13 feet). Hieronymus Bosch painted it in oil on oak around 1500.

**Did you know?** Fold the two side panels shut and the colors vanish. The outside shows the Earth as a gray, transparent globe half filled with water, probably the world on the third day of Creation, before animals and people. Opening it was meant to be a shock.

Triptychs usually stood on church altars. This one almost certainly never did: it was first recorded in 1517 in a nobleman's palace in Brussels, a showpiece for guests. It reads like a sentence, from left to right, and Bosch built in a few clues to guide you.

**[Composition lines]** Three clues to how the panels connect.

- *One horizon*: The left and center panels share the same horizon, the same grass, the same sky. The crowded garden in the middle grows straight out of Eden.
- *Where it breaks*: At this hinge the landscape stops. No horizon, no green, no daylight: the right panel is a night lit only by fires.
- *Three centers*: Each panel has a strange structure at its heart: a pink fountain, a blue globe, the Tree-Man. A fountain of life, then a fragile toy, then a hollow shell.

**Did you know?** Bosch was not his family name. He was born Jheronimus van Aken and signed with a short form of his hometown, 's-Hertogenbosch, in today's Netherlands. He spent nearly his whole life there, in a house on the market square.

So the story runs from a beginning to an end: Paradise, then a garden of pleasures, then punishment. That sounds simple. It gets less simple once you inspect Paradise itself, which ought to be the one peaceful place in the picture.

**[Guided tour]** God has just created Eve. From a distance, everything on the left panel looks calm.

- *The introduction*: God, shown young like Christ, holds Eve by the wrist and presents her to Adam, who has just woken up. Adam stares. Many viewers read that stare as the first spark of desire.
- *The owl*: In the dark hole at the base of the pink fountain sits an owl. In Bosch's time an owl was a night bird of ill omen as much as a wise one. It watches from the exact middle of Eden.
- *The cat*: A cat walks off with a small animal in its jaws. Nearby, creatures crawl out of a dark pond and birds squabble. Killing has already begun, before anyone has touched an apple.
- *The giraffe*: Bosch never saw a giraffe or an elephant. He likely copied them from travelers' drawings, which is why this one is white and slightly off. Eden is stocked with the rarest things he knew.

Paradise already holds a predator and a watching owl. Bosch seems to say the trouble did not begin in the garden next door: it was planted on the first day. With that in mind, the huge center panel becomes harder to judge.

Hundreds of naked men and women ride animals, bathe, nibble giant berries and climb into shells. Nobody is old, nobody works, nobody seems ashamed. No document tells us what Bosch meant, and scholars still disagree: a warning against lust, or a dream of the world as it might be if humans had never sinned?

Whichever you choose, the evidence is in the small things, and there are far too many for a guided walk. Roam the garden on your own. Watch for fruit and birds that are bigger than people, and for anything made of glass.

**[Free zoom]** 

- *The blue globe*: At the center of the lake floats a blue globe topped with pink spires. Tiny figures climb on it and sit in its openings. It looks grand and breakable at once.
- *The endless ride*: Women bathe in a round pool while men circle them on horses, boars, a camel and stranger beasts. Round and round, going nowhere: a merry-go-round of showing off.
- *Lovers in a bubble*: A couple sits inside a transparent sphere that grows from a plant like a soap bubble. A Dutch saying of the time compared happiness to glass: it breaks quickly.
- *Giant birds*: A kingfisher, a goldfinch and other birds tower over the bathers at the water's edge. The scale is flipped: here humans are the small animals.
- *The mussel shell*: A man carries a huge mussel shell on his back, with people shut inside it. Shells, pods and hollow fruit serve as hiding places all over the garden.
- *The owl again*: An owl perches above a ring of dancers. The bird that watched from the fountain in Eden has followed us into the garden.
- *The giant berry*: Figures cluster around a red berry almost as big as they are. Sweet, bright, and rotten within days: a neat picture of a pleasure that does not last.

**Key takeaway.** Everything in the garden is delicious and fragile: fruit that rots, glass that breaks, bubbles that burst. Whether Bosch disapproved or daydreamed, he painted pleasure as something that cannot last.

**Did you know?** For centuries nobody called it The Garden of Earthly Delights. Bosch left no title. Early Spanish descriptions name the picture after its fruit, as the painting of the strawberry, and the title we use settled in much later.

How did he keep so many tiny scenes bright and readable? Bosch worked in thin, almost transparent layers of oil paint over a white ground, which makes the colors glow like enamel. Technical scans show he changed his mind as he went, redrawing figures directly on the panel.

Then comes the bill. In the right panel the lights go out, and the same species that played in the garden is hunted, eaten and mocked. Bosch's cruelest idea is that the things people enjoyed become the tools that hurt them. Start with music.

**[Guided tour]** Three stops in the dark, from the top of the panel down.

- *Ears and a blade*: Two giant ears carry a knife between them like a war machine rolling over the damned. What it means is anyone's guess, and that is part of its power.
- *The music lesson*: Under a giant lute and harp, a sinner lies crushed, with a musical score written across his backside. The instruments of pleasure have become racks.
- *The bird-headed prince*: A blue monster with a bird's head and a cauldron for a hat sits on a tall chair, swallowing a person whole. He is often called the Prince of Hell.

**Did you know?** The notes on that sinner's backside are readable. In 2014 a music student in Oklahoma, Amelia Hamrick, transcribed them and posted a recording online. It spread around the world under an unofficial title: the butt song from Hell.

The painting soon became a prize. King Philip II of Spain, a devoted collector of Bosch, had it sent to his monastery-palace of El Escorial in 1593. It moved to the Prado in Madrid in the 1930s. Four centuries after Bosch, the Surrealists, painters of dreams such as Salvador Dalí, claimed him as an ancestor.

Return to the Tree-Man. Everyone else in Hell is too busy suffering to notice anything. He alone looks back, over his broken body, toward the garden and toward you. A warning, a regret, or the faint smile of a painter enjoying his own invention? Bosch never said.

## The Birth of Venus

*Alexandre Cabanel, 1863 · Musée d'Orsay, Paris · academic*

> An emperor bought it on sight. A young novelist said she was made of marzipan.

In the spring of 1863, one visitor to the big Paris art show liked this painting so much that he bought it for himself. The visitor was Napoleon III, emperor of the French. It is hard to imagine a better customer, or a clearer sign of what official France wanted from art.

The show was the Salon, the yearly exhibition where a jury decided which artists the public would see. That same year the jury turned away about three thousand works. The protests were so loud that the emperor allowed a separate show for the rejected. One of them was a picture by Édouard Manet with a naked woman at a picnic, and the crowds came to laugh at it.

**Key takeaway.** Same year, same city, two nudes: one bought by the emperor, one mocked by the crowd. The difference between them explains what “academic art” means.

Academic art is art made by the rules of the official schools: a noble subject taken from myth or history, perfect drawing, and a surface so smooth that no brush mark shows. Alexandre Cabanel was its star. Here the noble subject is Venus, Roman goddess of love, who according to the myth was born fully grown from the foam of the sea.

The myth was the alibi. A naked Parisian woman would have been indecent. A naked goddess was culture. Five putti, the chubby winged babies of old paintings, fly overhead and blow seashell trumpets to announce her. Still, something in her face is not quite as innocent as a newborn's.

**[Find it]** Venus seems asleep, one arm thrown over her forehead. Is she? Tap the spot that makes you doubt it.

- *Not quite asleep*: In the shadow of her arm, her eyelids are not fully shut. She seems to glance out at whoever is looking. The pose says “sleeping goddess”, the eyes say she knows you are there.

That small detail did not escape people at the time. The young writer Émile Zola, a friend of Manet, mocked this Venus as a pretty courtesan drowned in a river of milk, made not of flesh but of pink and white marzipan. He meant the paint itself: too sweet, too soft, too perfect to be a body.

**Did you know?** The year 1863 was very good to Cabanel. Besides the emperor's purchase, he was elected to the Académie des Beaux-Arts, the small club of artists that ruled French art, and became a professor at its famous school in Paris. For the next twenty-five years he trained hundreds of students and sat on the Salon jury.

Zola was unfair about one thing: this kind of surface is extremely hard to produce. Painters called it the “licked” finish. It took thin layers of oil paint, blended while wet with soft brushes until every transition disappeared. Compare three areas of the canvas to see where Cabanel hides his hand, and where he lets it show.

**[Guided tour]** The canvas is 130 by 225 cm (about 4 by 7.5 feet), and Venus is close to life-size. See how the paint changes from one zone to the next.

- *Skin like porcelain*: No vein, no crease, no body hair, no visible stroke. Light slides from hip to shoulder in one unbroken gradation. This is the marzipan Zola made fun of.
- *Foam*: Here the brush is allowed to show. Thicker white dabs make the froth she was born from, and her red-gold hair floats on it and trails out over the water.
- *The herald*: A putto blows into a seashell, wings out, cheeks puffed. His body dissolves a little into the pale sky, which keeps your attention on the goddess below.

After the smooth body, the sea feels almost rough. Cabanel knew what he was doing: a little texture around the figure makes her skin look even more polished. It is also why she seems to float on the wave more than lie in it. Nothing wet touches her.

**Did you know?** The picture was so popular that Cabanel painted it again. In 1875 an American collector, John Wolfe, ordered a smaller version, and it now hangs in the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York. Repeating a hit for a paying client was normal business for a successful Salon painter.

Then taste turned over. Manet and the Impressionists, laughed at in the 1860s, became the heroes of modern art, and painters like Cabanel were pushed into storage for most of the twentieth century. Today this Venus hangs in the Musée d'Orsay, a short walk from Manet's rejected picnic. Which one would you have bought in 1863? Be honest.

## The Death of Socrates

*Jacques-Louis David, 1787 · Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York · neoclassicism*

> The poison is ready. The old man would rather finish his sentence.

The young jailer cannot watch. He holds out the cup with one arm and hides his eyes with the other. In the cup is hemlock, a poison brewed from a plant. The old man on the bed is supposed to drink it. He goes on talking instead, one finger in the air, as if the cup were an interruption.

Athens, 399 BC. A jury has condemned the philosopher Socrates, about seventy years old, for disrespecting the gods and corrupting the young with his endless questions. He could have escaped. His friends had a plan ready. He refused: a man who spent his life teaching respect for the law would not run from it.

So this is a class, his last one. He is telling his followers that the soul does not die and that there is nothing to fear. They are falling apart. He is not. Jacques-Louis David builds the entire picture on that contrast, and on two gestures of the same man.

**[Composition lines]** Three lines hold the painting together.

- *The light*: Light falls from the upper left like a stage lamp. It ignores the grey wall and lands on Socrates's chest and white robe, the brightest things in the cell.
- *Up*: One arm shoots straight up. He points to where he believes he is going: a higher world of ideas, beyond the body.
- *Toward the cup*: The other hand reaches for the poison without a glance, and does not touch it yet. That small gap between fingers and cup is the tense center of the picture.

The gap was a decision. David could have shown Socrates drinking, or already dead. He chose the half second before, when the choice is made and nothing has happened yet. Tradition says a poet friend, André Chénier, advised him: let Socrates stay lost in his ideas and reach out only when he has finished speaking.

**Did you know?** Ancient writers describe the real Socrates as short, snub-nosed and pot-bellied, and he was seventy. David gave him the torso of an athlete. In this kind of painting the body displays the mind: firm character, firm shoulders.

The style has a name: Neoclassicism. It swept Europe in the late 1700s and looked back to ancient Greece and Rome for lessons in courage. Its rules are all here. A bare stone room. Figures lined up side by side like a carved relief. Sharp outlines, smooth paint with no visible brushwork, and nothing pretty for its own sake.

David did his homework. He read Plato's “Phaedo”, the ancient account of this day. In it, Socrates sends his wife Xanthippe home before the end, because her weeping is more than he wants to hear. David did not forget her.

**[Find it]** Xanthippe is on her way out. Where is she?

- *The staircase*: Deep in the background, small figures climb the stairs. One turns back and lifts a hand in farewell. She is usually read as Xanthippe: family life leaves, philosophy stays in the cell.

That staircase is the only exit in the painting, and David makes it small, dim and already behind everyone. In the foreground each follower grieves in his own way: one claws at the wall, one buries his face, one stares. A few of them are hiding something.

**[Guided tour]** Begin on the left, with the one man who is not looking.

- *Plato*: The old man at the foot of the bed, head bowed, is Plato, who wrote the story down. In reality he was a young man then, and by his own account he was absent that day, ill.
- *Scrolls and ink*: On the floor by his seat: rolled scrolls, an inkpot, a pen. A hint that the whole scene could be what Plato remembered, or imagined, when he wrote it years later.
- *Crito's hand*: Crito, the old friend who had offered to arrange an escape, grips his teacher's thigh and looks up at him, as if he could still hold him back.
- *The stone block*: David signed on the block Crito sits on, and put his initials on Plato's bench as well. Some read it as the painter taking a seat with the two closest followers.

So the witness who records everything was never in the room, and David knew it. Seating Plato there as an old man turns the canvas into something stranger than a report. It becomes a memory, replayed in a writer's mind long afterward. That may be why everything looks so clear and so still.

**Did you know?** Thomas Jefferson, then the American envoy in Paris, saw the canvas at the Salon of 1787, the big public art exhibition held at the Louvre. He wrote to a friend that it was the best thing in the show.

One object is easy to miss in the whole image: the open shackles and chain lying on the floor under the bed. Socrates has just been unchained. He is free to move, and he stays where he is.

**Did you know?** For all its grandeur, the canvas is not huge: about 1.3 by 2 meters (51 by 77 inches). It was painted for a private client, a wealthy young judge named Trudaine de la Sablière, and has been in New York since the Metropolitan Museum bought it in 1931.

**Key takeaway.** David stops the clock just before the act. His subject is not a death but a choice: a man who would rather die than give up what he thinks is right.

Two years later the French Revolution broke out. David threw himself into it, sat in the assembly and voted for the death of the king. Was this picture, painted in the Paris of Louis XVI, already a call to put principles above your own life? Or did the Revolution lend it that meaning afterward? Historians have not settled it.

## Liberty Leading the People

*Eugène Delacroix, 1830 · Louvre, Paris · romanticism*

> Delacroix did not fight on the barricades. He painted this instead.

Paris, July 27, 1830. King Charles X has just shut down the free press and dissolved the elected chamber. Printers walk out. By evening, stones are flying. July 28: streets are blocked with paving stones, carts and furniture, and the army fires. July 29: soldiers change sides, the palace falls. Three days. The king flees the country.

Eugène Delacroix, 32, already a famous painter, watched it happen and did not fight. That autumn he wrote to his brother that if he had not fought for his country, he would at least paint for her. He finished this huge canvas in about three months.

It measures 2.6 by 3.25 meters (roughly 8.5 by 10.5 feet), so the figures in front are life-size. A barricade is a wall thrown up across a street. Delacroix puts you at the foot of one, exactly where the crowd is about to land. They are coming over the top, toward you.

**[Composition lines]** A riot is chaos. Yet this picture is easy to read in one second. Here is the hidden order.

- *A pyramid*: The dead form the base, the fighters the sides, and the flag the tip. A triangle is the most stable shape there is. Delacroix uses it to hold a stampede together.
- *The rising line*: From her bare foot on the rubble to her fist on the flagpole, one long upward push. Your eye climbs with it and ends on the colors.
- *A look upward*: The wounded figure at her feet lifts their head toward her face. That gaze tells you where to look, and what she means to the people around her.

At the top of the pyramid stands someone who was not there in July. This woman is an allegory, an idea given a human body: Liberty herself. Her profile is straight out of an ancient Greek statue, and her red cap is the Phrygian cap, worn as a sign of freedom since the French Revolution of 1789.

**Key takeaway.** Delacroix mixes two things painters had kept apart: a goddess-like symbol and real people in dirty street clothes, in the same smoke, on the same stones.

**Did you know?** The blue, white and red flag had been banned in France for fifteen years. Under the returned kings, the official flag was plain white. Seeing the three colors fly again over Paris in July 1830 was a shock in itself, and Delacroix makes them the brightest patch of paint in the picture.

Around her, Delacroix chose his fighters with care. No two belong to the same world. He wanted the uprising to look like the work of a whole city, not of one class.

**[Guided tour]** Who follows Liberty? Meet four of them, from left to right.

- *The worker*: Open shirt, apron strap across his chest, a saber in his fist. He is a factory hand or craftsman, the kind of man who built most of the barricades.
- *The man in the top hat*: Jacket, tie and hat: city clothes, with a hunting gun. People long said this was Delacroix himself. Historians today doubt it.
- *The kneeling figure*: Blue smock, red sash, a bit of white shirt: the flag again, worn on a body. The colors of the flag are scattered all over the canvas like an echo.
- *The boy*: A street kid in a student's black cap, a pistol in each hand, mouth open in a shout. He is the only one running ahead of Liberty.

That boy had a long life ahead of him. More than thirty years later, Victor Hugo created Gavroche, the brave street child of his novel Les Misérables, and readers have linked the two ever since. As for the rest of the crowd, behind these four it dissolves into smoke, hats and bayonets: a few faces stand for thousands.

Where exactly are we? Delacroix invented this barricade, but he wanted no doubt about the city. He slipped a famous Paris landmark into the smoke, and on top of it, a tiny sign that the rebels are winning.

**[Find it]** Somewhere in the haze stands the cathedral of Notre-Dame. Find its two towers.

- *Notre-Dame*: The twin towers rise above the rooftops on the right. A tricolor flag, just a fleck of paint, flies from one of them: the cathedral has been taken.

The lower third is the hardest part to look at. A man stripped of his trousers on the left, two dead royal soldiers on the right. Delacroix gives the bodies of both sides the same weight. Liberty has to step over all of them. The picture cheers the revolution and counts its cost in the same breath.

**Did you know?** The new government bought the painting in 1831 for 3,000 francs, then got nervous. A picture that makes revolt look glorious is awkward for whoever is in power. It was taken off view and spent years out of sight, part of that time back with Delacroix's family. It only entered the Louvre in 1874, after his death.

Many critics in 1831 were not kind. They found Liberty dirty and vulgar, more market woman than goddess. That was exactly the point. Delacroix belonged to Romanticism, a movement that preferred emotion, movement and color to calm, polished perfection. His brushwork stays loose, and the smoke is paint dragged quickly across the canvas.

**Did you know?** For years, French people carried this painting in their wallets: it was printed on the 100-franc banknote, next to Delacroix's portrait. And in 2024 the real canvas came back from a long cleaning. Layers of yellowed varnish were removed, and the sky turned out to be far bluer than anyone alive had seen.

## Maestà

*Duccio, 1311 · Museo dell'Opera del Duomo, Siena · gothic*

> A wall of gold, painted on both sides, that a whole city carried through its streets.

Begin with the wood. Poplar planks, glued and nailed into a wall about five meters high (16 feet), then covered on both faces with paint and real gold. When it left Duccio's workshop in June 1311, Siena had never seen a painting this big.

The image in front of you rebuilds the front as it once stood. In the middle, the main panel, roughly 2.1 by 4 meters (7 by 13 feet). Below it runs a strip of small stories called a predella, the base of an altarpiece. On top, a crown of pointed gables.

**Did you know?** On June 9, 1311, Siena closed its shops for the day. The bishop, the priests, the city officials and a crowd holding candles walked the finished painting from the workshop to the cathedral while every bell in town rang.

Why such a fuss over a painting? Siena considered the Virgin Mary its personal protector. Half a century earlier, before a battle against Florence that it had little chance of winning, the city had handed her its keys. It won. A “Maestà”, Italian for “majesty”, shows her as a queen on her throne, holding court.

So this is a state portrait as much as a religious one, and the painter knew how much it mattered. Artists of his time almost never signed their work. Duccio did, and he chose the most visible spot he could without touching the holy figures.

**[Find it]** Duccio wrote his own name on this painting. Where would a painter dare to put it?

- *The step of the throne*: A band of red letters runs around the base, right under Mary's feet. It is a prayer in Latin, and Duccio's name is inside it.

The prayer says, roughly: Holy Mother of God, give Siena peace, and give Duccio life, because he painted you like this. One line for the city, one line for himself. A craftsman, paid by the day, is asking for a reward in the same breath as his government.

**Key takeaway.** With the Maestà, a painter stops being an anonymous workman. He signs, he bargains with heaven, and a whole city walks behind his work.

The paint is tempera: colored powder mixed with egg yolk. It dries in minutes, so there is no blending on the panel. Every soft cheek is built from hundreds of hair-thin strokes. The background is gold leaf, real gold beaten thinner than paper, then polished with a smooth stone until it shines like metal.

Now the hard part. Duccio has to fit a queen, a child, a throne and a crowd of angels and saints into one rectangle, and it must stay calm enough to pray in front of. His answer is almost architectural.

**[Composition lines]** Three simple lines hold this whole crowd in order.

- *The axis*: Mary sits exactly in the middle, and far larger than anyone else. In medieval painting, size shows rank, not distance.
- *Rows of halos*: Angels and saints stand in ranks, heads at the same height like a choir. The gold discs repeat across the panel as a steady rhythm.
- *The kneeling four*: Four figures kneel in front, two on each side. They are Siena's own patron saints, placed nearest to us, speaking to Mary for the city.

That strict order comes from Byzantine art, the style of icons from the eastern Mediterranean: gold, symmetry, stillness. What Duccio adds is softness. Heads tilt. Angels lean their chins on the throne like children at a windowsill. The gold edge of Mary's dark cloak wanders down her body in one loose, curling line.

**Did you know?** Siena's archives show Duccio being fined again and again: for debts, for breaking the curfew, for refusing to turn up for military service. The city kept giving him its biggest commissions anyway.

Remember the two painted faces. The back, not shown here, carried the Passion, the last days of Jesus, in 26 scenes. Citizens in the nave saw the queen. The priests, seated behind the altar, saw the story. The contract of 1308 gave Duccio one job only, and he spent about three years on it.

Even on the front, the edges are full of stories. The strip below tells the childhood of Jesus, each scene separated by a prophet holding a scroll. The gables above tell the last days of Mary. Wander through them.

**[Free zoom]** 

- *The Annunciation*: The story begins at the far left: an angel steps through an archway to tell Mary she will have a child. The little building is tilted to show us its inside.
- *A prophet*: Between two scenes stands a man with a scroll. Prophets from the Old Testament act as footnotes, announcing what the next picture shows.
- *The Nativity*: Mary lies on a red cushion in a cave under a wooden shelter. She is still the biggest figure, even lying down.
- *The Child*: Wrapped in a thin pink cloth, he sits on his mother's arm like a real, heavy baby. Her hand closes around his leg to hold him.
- *Leaning angels*: Angels rest on the back of the marble throne and peer over it. A small, human gesture inside a very formal picture.
- *A kneeling bishop*: One of Siena's four patron saints, in a white and gold robe, with his bishop's staff. He looks up at Mary on the city's behalf.
- *Mary's last days*: In the gables, the apostles gather around Mary's bed. These scenes close the story that the strip at the bottom opened.
- *Gable angels*: At the very top, an angel in each pointed frame. The gilded spires echo the Gothic cathedral the painting was made for.

In those small scenes Duccio is freest. Rooms open like doll houses, people argue, sleep, travel. Younger Sienese painters such as Simone Martini and the Lorenzetti brothers learned storytelling from these panels, and Siena's gentle, colorful manner stayed recognizable for two centuries.

The painting's own story is rougher. In 1506 it was taken down from the high altar, out of fashion. In 1771 it was sawn apart: front split from back, big panels cut into pieces, small ones sold off one by one.

**Did you know?** Pieces of the Maestà now hang in London, New York, Washington, Madrid and Fort Worth, Texas. A few panels have never been found, so nobody alive has seen the whole thing.

## The Hunters in the Snow

*Pieter Bruegel the Elder, 1565 · Kunsthistorisches Museum, Vienna · northern_renaissance*

> Three hunters, a dozen dogs, one small fox. Winter has never been painted colder.

The sky is the color of a frozen pond. No wind, no sun, no shadows. Snow swallows every sound except, perhaps, the crunch of boots and the crackle of one fire. You can almost feel your fingers going numb. Very few paintings give you a temperature this quickly.

Pieter Bruegel the Elder painted it in 1565, in what is now Belgium. The winter that had just ended was one of the hardest of the century there: rivers froze solid and stayed frozen for weeks. Bruegel was not imagining the cold. He and everyone he knew had just lived through it.

**Did you know?** Europe was then entering what historians call the Little Ice Age, several centuries of colder winters. Frozen canals became a normal part of life in the Low Countries, and winter scenes with skaters became a local specialty that painters kept producing for a hundred years after Bruegel.

How do you paint cold? Bruegel does it with almost no colors: white, black-brown, and one icy gray-green that he uses twice, for the sky and for the ice, as if one were the mirror of the other. Against that, the men, dogs and trees are dark cutouts. Now, the title promises hunters. So what did they catch?

**[Find it]** Three men and a pack of dogs are coming home. Their whole catch is hanging on one man's back. Can you spot it?

- *One thin fox*: A single fox dangles from the spear over this hunter's shoulder. That is all, for three men and a dozen dogs. The day has been long and poor.

Once you have seen the fox, the whole group changes. The men walk bent forward, and we see none of their faces. The dogs trail behind with their heads and tails down. Nobody at the inn turns to greet them. Bruegel tells you about hunger and tiredness using only backs.

**Key takeaway.** Bruegel made ordinary people the subject of large, ambitious paintings, at a time when that size was normally reserved for saints, kings and ancient heroes.

The picture was one of a set showing the times of the year, ordered by Niclaes Jonghelinck, a rich Antwerp banker, for his country house. There were probably six panels, and five survive. Three hang together in Vienna, including this one, which stands for the deepest part of winter.

It is painted in oil on oak boards, 117 by 162 cm (about 46 by 64 inches). On that fairly modest surface Bruegel fits a hilltop, two villages, a wide valley and a mountain range. The trick is in how he leads your eye from one to the next.

**[Composition lines]** You are standing on a hilltop, just behind the hunters. Follow the route your eye takes into the valley.

- *The hunters' path*: The dogs, then the three men, each one smaller and farther than the last. They are walking away from you, so you automatically walk with them.
- *The edge of the hill*: The snowy ridge cuts the picture on a diagonal. Everything on this side is near and large. Beyond it, the ground drops away and the valley opens far below.
- *The way out*: From the ponds your eye zigzags past the church, across the white fields and up to the sharp peaks. The picture runs for miles.

The four dark tree trunks do a similar job. They stand like posts along a road, each one thinner than the one before, and they measure the distance for you. Bruegel also places you high up, like a bird. This raised view lets him show near and far at once, the hunters at your feet and a whole country beyond them.

**Did you know?** Those jagged mountains have nothing to do with Belgium, one of the flattest places in Europe. About twelve years earlier Bruegel had traveled to Italy and crossed the Alps on the way. He drew them, kept the drawings, and went on slipping Alpine peaks into Flemish landscapes for the rest of his life.

So far the picture is about three tired men. But Bruegel was famous for something else: filling a scene with dozens of tiny lives, each busy with its own affairs. Down in the valley, winter is not only hardship. It is also work, and games.

**[Guided tour]** Leave the hunters on their hill. Three stops below, from the nearest to the farthest.

- *The fire at the inn*: A family feeds a straw fire outside the inn, probably to singe the bristles off a pig. It is the only warm color in the whole painting, and the flames lean with the wind.
- *A load of sticks*: A figure crosses the little bridge under a big bundle of firewood. In a winter like this one, keeping the house warm is a daily job.
- *The frozen ponds*: Dozens of tiny black figures skate, pull each other along, and play games with sticks on the ice. Each is only a few strokes of dark paint, and yet you can tell who is falling.

This is what makes the painting so rich. The same cold that sends the hunters home nearly empty-handed gives the village children a playground. Bruegel does not choose between the two. He simply shows both, from far enough away that no one is the hero.

**Did you know?** The banker did not enjoy his paintings for long. In 1566, only a year after they were finished, Jonghelinck offered his art collection, Bruegels included, as a guarantee for a friend's unpaid debt to the city of Antwerp. The pictures later passed to the Habsburg rulers, which is why three of them are in Vienna today.

Bruegel died in 1569, just four years after this winter. He left about forty paintings, and two sons who became painters and copied his inventions for decades. The snowy village seen from above, with skaters and bare trees, became one of the most repeated images in European art. You have seen its descendants on Christmas cards.

**[Free zoom]** 

- *The crooked sign*: The inn's sign hangs askew, holding on by one corner. Small signs of neglect like this add to the feeling that times are hard here.
- *The gliding bird*: A bird hangs in the air with its wings spread, a black shape against the green sky. It marks the empty space between hill and mountains, and makes the drop feel deep.
- *The pack*: Greyhounds, a red hound, small curly-tailed dogs: no two are alike. Their lowered heads tell the story of the day as clearly as the fox does.
- *The frozen mill*: Long icicles hang where water should be running under the mill. Its wheel cannot turn, so no grain can be ground here until the thaw.
- *A ride on the ice*: One figure tows another across the ice on a small sled. A spot of red clothing is enough to catch your eye from across the room.
- *Borrowed mountains*: Sharp, rocky peaks that Bruegel remembered from his crossing of the Alps, set down behind a Flemish village.

Four centuries later, the Russian director Andrei Tarkovsky put this painting in his science fiction film Solaris. Aboard a space station, a character stares at it for a long time: it stands for Earth, for home. Go back to that green sky and the crunch of snow. A picture of one hard winter in 1565 has become many people's picture of winter itself.

## The Blue Boy

*Thomas Gainsborough, 1770 · Huntington Library, San Marino · rococo*

> Legend says this boy was painted in blue to win an argument. The dates say otherwise.

The story goes like this. Sir Joshua Reynolds, the most powerful painter in England, declared that blue is a cold color and must never fill the middle of a picture. His rival Thomas Gainsborough heard about it, grinned, and painted a boy in blue from collar to shoe bows, just to prove him wrong.

It is a wonderful story, and the dates ruin it. Gainsborough exhibited the painting in London in 1770. Reynolds gave his lecture about warm and cold colors in 1778, eight years later. If one of them was answering the other, it was not Gainsborough.

So why all that blue? The true hero of this picture had been dead for over a century: Anthony van Dyck, the Flemish painter who made English aristocrats look effortlessly elegant in the 1630s. Gainsborough adored him. The boy's outfit is not the fashion of 1770 at all. It is a costume.

**[Guided tour]** This suit was about 130 years out of date when Gainsborough painted it. Follow it from the neck down, then come back up to the face.

- *Lace collar*: A wide lace collar spills over the jacket, straight out of Van Dyck's portraits. Up close it is only flicks and dabs of white: lace suggested without a single thread being drawn.
- *Slashed sleeve*: The sleeve is cut open to show white linen, a 17th-century luxury. The satin is built from long strokes of blue, slate, turquoise and white laid side by side, never blended smooth.
- *Shoe bows*: Even the shoes get big blue ribbon rosettes. From head to foot, one color, in a dozen shades.
- *The face*: After all that dashing brushwork, the face is painted softly and with care. Pink cheeks, lips closed, a level look. He is playing a cavalier, and he knows it.

That quick, streaky handling was Gainsborough's trademark. He liked very long brushes and thin, fluid paint, and he worked on the whole canvas at once so that nothing went stiff. From across a room the strokes fuse into shining satin. One step closer, they fall apart into ribbons of paint.

**Did you know?** X-rays show the canvas was secondhand. Gainsborough had begun a portrait of an older man on it, gave up, and painted the boy on top. He also painted a fluffy white dog at the boy's feet, then changed his mind and buried it under a pile of rocks.

A reused canvas suggests that nobody ordered this picture. Gainsborough earned his living flattering paying clients in the spa town of Bath, and he seems to have made this one for himself, to show London what he could do. Which brings up a second legend: who is the boy?

For two hundred years the answer was Jonathan Buttall, son of a rich hardware merchant, who did own the picture early on. Many specialists now doubt it and suggest the painter's nephew and assistant, Gainsborough Dupont. No record settles it. Whoever he is, he poses like a prince, and one prop completes the act, half swallowed by the dusk.

**[Find it]** He has politely taken off his hat. Where is it?

- *The plumed hat*: A wide black hat, nearly invisible against the dark landscape, given away by its white plume. Hat in hand, fist on hip: this is how gentlemen stand in Van Dyck's portraits of the English court.

Hat low, elbow out, stormy sky behind: every signal says nobility, though the model was most likely a tradesman's son or a nephew. In a society obsessed with rank, Gainsborough shows that grandeur can be put on like a suit. The canvas is 1.78 meters tall (about 5 ft 10 in), so the boy meets you nearly life-size.

**Did you know?** In 1921 the Duke of Westminster sold the picture to the American railroad magnate Henry E. Huntington for about $728,000, reported at the time as the highest price ever paid for a painting. British newspapers treated the sale as a national loss.

Before it sailed, the National Gallery in London hung it for three weeks, and some 90,000 people came to say goodbye. The gallery's director wrote two words on the back: “Au revoir.” Then the painting crossed the Atlantic and a continent to California, where it has lived ever since.

**Key takeaway.** The Blue Boy is a portrait in disguise: a painting of 1770 dressed as one from the 1630s, made by an artist showing how much a brush can do with a single color.

“Au revoir” means “until we meet again,” and it took a while. In January 2022, one hundred years to the day after the farewell, the painting returned to the National Gallery for a short visit. A few months earlier the Huntington had hung a reply across the room: Kehinde Wiley's portrait of a young Black man in the same pose, in a tie-dye shirt and shorts.

## The Potato Eaters

*Vincent van Gogh, 1885 · Van Gogh Museum, Amsterdam · postimp*

> Before the sunflowers, Van Gogh bet everything on five people and a dish of potatoes.

Evening in Nuenen, a farming village in the south of the Netherlands, 1885. A thin man of 32 sits in the corner of a cottage with a sketchbook on his knee. The family at the table has stopped noticing him. He draws a hand, then a jaw, then the same hand again, until the lamp runs low.

Vincent van Gogh had been teaching himself to paint for barely five years. All that winter he had drawn and painted farm workers, around forty heads, plus hands, bonnets and coffee pots. Now he wanted to put everything he had learned into one ambitious picture with several figures. He painted the final canvas in his studio, mostly from memory.

His father, the village pastor, had died suddenly a few weeks before. The house was tense, money came only from his brother Theo in Paris, and not one painting had sold. This picture was meant to be the proof that he was a real painter.

The subject could not be plainer: five people, a dish of potatoes, a pot of coffee. And one oil lamp. Nothing in the room is visible except by that small flame, so the lamp ends up arranging the whole picture.

**[Composition lines]** Start from the flame and see where the light goes.

- *Light to the left*: The glow lands on the man's cheekbone and on the young woman's wide-open face. Away from the lamp, their backs sink into dark green.
- *Light to the right*: It catches the second man's brow and the old woman's lowered eyes under her white cap. Four faces, each lit from a slightly different angle.
- *The chain of hands*: Below the faces, a second line: a fork in the dish, a hand offering a cup, another pouring. Nobody speaks, but the hands pass things along.

Those hands are the real subject. Van Gogh explained to Theo that he wanted viewers to understand one thing: the fingers reaching into the dish are the same fingers that dug the potatoes out of the ground. These people have earned their meal honestly.

**Key takeaway.** Van Gogh was not painting poverty to make you pity it. He was painting work, and the dignity of eating what you have grown yourself.

**Did you know?** To describe the color he gave the faces, Van Gogh compared it to a good dusty potato, unpeeled. He mixed his browns and greens so the people would look made of the same earth they farmed.

He also made them rough on purpose. Bony knuckles, heavy noses, ears that stick out. Pictures of country life sold well at the time, but they tended to be sweet and tidy. Van Gogh felt a peasant painting should smell of bacon, smoke and steam, not of perfume.

**Did you know?** He sent a print of the composition to his painter friend Anthon van Rappard, who answered with a list of faults: the arm too short, the nose wrong, the whole thing careless. Vincent mailed the letter back. They never repaired the friendship.

Theo, an art dealer, could not sell it either. Paris had fallen for the bright colors of the Impressionists, and this was the darkest thing imaginable. A year later Vincent joined him there, and his palette lit up. Yet in 1887, surrounded by all that color, he still told his sister this was the best thing he had done.

**Did you know?** In April 1991, thieves took twenty paintings from the Van Gogh Museum in a single night, this one among them. All were found the same day in an abandoned car.

The canvas is not large, about 82 by 114 centimeters (32 by 45 inches), and on a screen it can look like a brown fog. It is not. Van Gogh furnished this room object by object, and the dark gives them up slowly. Turn your brightness up and go looking.

**[Free zoom]** 

- *The clock*: High in the left corner, a wall clock with a round face. It is evening: the day's work is over and this is the only meal that is not rushed.
- *A print on the wall*: A small framed picture hangs beside the clock. You can just make out a cross: a Crucifixion, the one decoration in the house.
- *The lamp*: An oil lamp under a pale shade, with a flame the size of a fingernail. It is the brightest and warmest touch of paint on the canvas.
- *The girl*: We only see her back, a dark shape in the middle of the family. Steam from the dish rises around her and catches the light along her edges.
- *The potatoes*: One shared dish of steaming potatoes, cut into pieces. No plates: each person takes from the middle with a fork.
- *The coffee*: The old woman pours a dark drink into small bowls lined up on the table. It is the meal's one small luxury.
- *The old woman*: Her eyes are down, fixed on the pot. The white cap is built from thick, short strokes of gray and green, with almost no real white in it.
- *The man in profile*: A sharp cheekbone, a cap pulled low. He stares past the woman beside him, who looks at him and gets no answer.

## The Burial of the Count of Orgaz

*El Greco, 1586 · Santo Tomé, Toledo · mannerism*

> Two saints came down to bury a generous man. Toledo's gentlemen barely blinked.

In Toledo they tell this story. In 1323 a nobleman named Gonzalo Ruiz died. He had been generous to the poor and to his parish church, Santo Tomé. At his funeral, two saints came down from heaven, lifted the body with their own hands, and laid it in the tomb. Then they vanished.

More than 250 years later, the priest of Santo Tomé had a problem. The lord had ordered his town, Orgaz, to give money and food to the church every year, forever. The townspeople had stopped paying. The priest went to court, won, and decided to celebrate with a painting above the tomb. He asked a parishioner: a Greek painter the locals called El Greco.

**Did you know?** The “Count” of Orgaz was never a count. Gonzalo Ruiz was lord of the town. His family only received the title about two centuries after his death, and the painting's name promoted him after the fact.

The canvas is enormous: about 4.8 meters high and 3.6 wide (roughly 16 by 12 feet), rounded at the top to fit the chapel wall. El Greco had to show a funeral on earth and a welcome in heaven at once. So he cut the picture in two. Where exactly does one world stop and the other begin?

**[Composition lines]** Three lines hold this crowded picture together.

- *The frontier*: A row of heads runs from edge to edge like a fence. Below it: black clothes, real faces, weight. Above it: clouds that look like ice, and bodies that float.
- *The way up*: From the dead man's face, the path climbs through an angel in the clouds and ends at Christ, in white, at the very top. The whole painting is a route for one soul.
- *The court*: Christ, Mary on the left, John the Baptist on the right. Together they form a triangle: a judge and two people pleading for the newcomer.

Start at the bottom, where the miracle happens. The priest's contract was precise: it asked for the two saints holding the body, and for people watching. El Greco painted this lower half so that you could almost touch it. Gold thread, cold steel, lace.

**[Guided tour]** Four stops around the body, from left to right and down.

- *Saint Stephen*: The young one. He holds the legs. He wears a dalmatic, the stiff golden robe of a deacon, a church assistant. His face is calm, almost tender.
- *Saint Augustine*: The old one, with a white beard and a bishop's pointed hat, called a miter. He takes the shoulders. Two saints from two different centuries, working as pallbearers.
- *The dead lord*: His face is gray, his body bends like a bow. He is buried in black armor that shines like a mirror. Painting polished metal was a way for an artist to show off, and El Greco does.
- *A picture in the picture*: On the hem of Stephen's robe, the embroidery shows men throwing stones at a kneeling figure. It is Stephen's own death. The saint wears his story on his clothes.

Now the men in black. The funeral took place in 1323, yet nobody here is dressed for 1323. The white ruffs, the pointed beards, the red crosses of a knightly order: this is Toledo in the 1580s. El Greco filled the miracle with portraits of his neighbors, as if it had happened last week.

**Did you know?** When the painting was done, the priest found the price too high and asked for a second valuation. The new experts set it even higher. After a long quarrel, El Greco settled for the first figure: 1,200 ducats. He argued about money over nearly every big commission of his life.

Almost all of these gentlemen look sideways, or down, or inward. They attend a miracle the way you attend a meeting. But one of them does something different, and once you catch it you cannot unsee it.

**[Find it]** One man in the row of mourners looks straight at you. Where is he?

- *The man who sees you*: Just above the two saints, a face turned fully to the front, eyes on yours. A painter who puts himself in a crowd usually looks out like this, because he worked from a mirror.

Many historians think that this face is El Greco himself. No document proves it. One family member, however, is certain, and he stands much lower, at the left edge, far too young for this company.

**[Guided tour]** A child in black is the only person here whose job is to talk to you.

- *Jorge Manuel*: This is El Greco's son, about eight years old. Like the man above, he looks out of the picture. He is your guide.
- *His finger*: His left hand points at the miracle, toward the embroidered rose on Stephen's sleeve. The message is simple: this is what you should be looking at.
- *The handkerchief*: A white cloth sticks out of his pocket. On it El Greco signed in Greek letters and wrote 1578. That is not the year of the painting. It is the year the boy was born.

A signature in Greek, in the middle of Spain. El Greco was born Domenikos Theotokopoulos on the island of Crete, where he learned to paint icons, the gold-backed holy images of the Eastern church. He moved to Venice, then to Rome, and reached Toledo around 1577. He never left, and he never stopped signing in Greek.

**Key takeaway.** El Greco is the great name of Mannerism in Spain: a style that stretches bodies, twists poses and uses strange, cold colors, because feeling matters more than copying nature.

That style takes over as soon as you cross the frontier of heads. Upstairs, nothing obeys the rules of downstairs. Limbs grow too long. Yellow turns sour, blue turns electric. There is no sky and no ground, only folds of cloud. Wander around heaven and compare it with the solid men below.

**[Free zoom]** 

- *The soul*: A blond angel pushes a pale, misty shape up through a narrow gap in the clouds. It is the dead man's soul, shown small and soft like a newborn. Dying, here, looks like being born.
- *Mary*: In red and blue, she leans down with an open hand, ready to receive the soul. Her eyes are lowered toward the angel climbing up to her.
- *John the Baptist*: Thin, half naked, kneeling on a cloud. His body is far too long to be real. He pleads with Christ on behalf of the newcomer.
- *The keys*: Two keys dangle from the hand of an old man in yellow. He is Saint Peter, keeper of the gates of heaven. They hang right above the mourners, almost within reach.
- *Christ*: The brightest spot of the canvas. His white robe is the source of light for everything above the clouds. His arm reaches down to the left, toward Peter and his keys.
- *The blessed*: A packed crowd of profiles, all turned toward Christ. Heaven has its own audience, a mirror of the row of gentlemen below.
- *The harp*: At the far left edge, a figure holds a harp. He is usually read as King David, the musician king of the Bible.
- *The priest in white*: Back on earth: a priest in a see-through white tunic turns his back to you and tilts his head up. Among all the men below, he is one of the very few who notice that the sky has opened.

**Did you know?** The painting has never moved. It still hangs in Santo Tomé, on the wall it was made for, directly above the lord's real tomb. Stand in front of it and the saints seem to lower the body out of the canvas and into the stone beneath.

After El Greco died in 1614, taste changed, and for almost three centuries his long bodies and sour colors were treated as mistakes, even as signs of bad eyesight. Around 1900, young painters saw something else: an artist who bent reality on purpose. Picasso studied him closely.

**Key takeaway.** A local legend, a lawsuit and a row of neighbors: El Greco turned a parish matter into a picture of two worlds, and let a child show you the door between them.

## Portrait of Pablo Picasso

*Juan Gris, 1912 · Art Institute of Chicago · cubism*

> A young painter's thank-you to his famous neighbor, built entirely out of tilted planes.

Two Spaniards lived in the same shabby studio building on the hill of Montmartre in Paris. One, Pablo Picasso, was already the talk of the avant-garde. The other, Juan Gris, six years younger, watched what his neighbor was doing through the open doors and learned. In 1912 he painted this to say thank you.

Gris had arrived from Madrid in 1906, aged 19, with almost no money. For years he paid the rent by drawing cartoons for humor magazines. He only began painting seriously around 1910. This portrait was among the first pictures he dared to show in public, and he chose the biggest subject available.

**Did you know?** “Juan Gris” was an invented name. He was born José Victoriano González-Pérez. Gris means “gray” in Spanish, a modest choice for a painter who became known for cool, silvery color.

The style is Cubism, which Picasso and Georges Braque had developed in the years just before. A Cubist painter does not show a thing from one spot. He breaks it into flat facets seen from several angles and fits them back together on the canvas. The sitter can get hard to read. So Gris leaves one clear clue to the man's profession.

**[Find it]** Picasso is shown as a painter. Where is the proof?

- *The palette*: He holds a palette, thumb hooked through the hole. On it sit four dabs of paint: blue, yellow, red and black. They are almost the only pure colors in the whole canvas.

Everything else is blue-gray, cream and brown, as if lit by a cold window. That restraint is deliberate. Early Cubists set strong color aside so that nothing would distract from the structure. Gris then makes the joke that the painter's wild colors are all still on the palette, waiting.

Now compare this with what Picasso himself was painting in 1912: dense, flickering pictures where the figure nearly dissolves. Gris is different. You can find the head, the collar, the hands at once. He was trained in technical drawing, and it shows in the way he organizes the surface.

**[Composition lines]** Trace the hidden order behind the fragments.

- *A slanting background*: The wall behind Picasso is sliced by diagonals falling from upper right to lower left. Each band is light on one side and shaded on the other, like a folded sheet of paper.
- *The same angle again*: The slant repeats, parallel to the first, all the way toward the head. The repetition gives the picture a steady beat instead of chaos.
- *The buttons*: The jacket buttons march down the opposite way, upper left to lower right. Body and background cross like the two strokes of an X, which locks the figure in place.

The face follows the same logic. One eye looks at you from the front. The cheek and jaw beside it swing round as if seen from the side. You are given a walk around Picasso's head in a single image, yet the heavy fringe of hair across his forehead still makes him recognizable.

**Key takeaway.** Gris showed that Cubism could be clear. He kept its broken, many-sided view, and added order, light and a likeness you can still read.

**Did you know?** In the bottom right corner Gris wrote a dedication in French, “Hommage à Pablo Picasso,” above his signature. The hero was not entirely flattered. According to friends, Picasso stayed cool toward the younger man who had learned his language so fast.

The public noticed, though. Shown in Paris in the spring of 1912, the portrait announced Gris as the third major name of Cubism beside Picasso and Braque. He had fifteen years left: he died in 1927, at 40. The canvas, about 93 centimeters tall (37 inches), now hangs in the Art Institute of Chicago.

**Key takeaway.** A tribute can also be a quiet challenge. Gris painted his teacher in the teacher's own style, and made the style his own while doing it.

## Fine Wind, Clear Morning (Red Fuji)

*Katsushika Hokusai, 1831 · Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York · ukiyoe*

> For a few minutes at dawn, Japan's sacred mountain turns the color of brick.

Red is not the color of Mount Fuji. Most of the year the mountain is gray-blue, or white with snow. But in late summer and early autumn, when the snow has almost gone and the sky is clear, the first sun can light the bare slopes for a few minutes. People in Japan have a name for that sight: Red Fuji.

The real title says nothing about red. It describes weather: a fine southern wind, a clear morning. Hokusai gives you three things only. A mountain, a sky, a forest. No people, no houses, no road. So how does so little hold your eye for so long?

**[Composition lines]** The answer is in two slopes that do not match.

- *The long climb*: The left slope starts at the edge of the paper and rises in one slow curve, getting steeper as it goes. Your eye travels it like a hiker, from the forest to the snow.
- *The short drop*: The right slope is cut off by the border almost at once. The summit sits far to the right, not in the middle. That imbalance leaves the whole left half to the sky.

**Key takeaway.** Hokusai pushes his subject to one side and gives the empty part as much weight as the full part. In Japanese design, the space left open is part of the picture.

This is not a painting. It is a woodblock print, made around 1831 by a team. Hokusai drew the design. A carver cut it into planks of cherry wood, one plank for each color. A printer brushed pigment on each plank and pressed sheets of paper on it by hand, rubbing the back. A publisher paid for everything and sold the result.

**Did you know?** Prints like this one were cheap. In Edo, the city now called Tokyo, a sheet is often said to have cost about as much as a double helping of noodles. Thousands were pulled, until the wood wore down.

The deep blue of the sky was a novelty. It is Prussian blue, a chemical pigment invented in Europe, which had recently become affordable in Japan. It was stronger than the plant blues used before and did not fade as fast. Hokusai's publisher built a whole series around it: the Thirty-Six Views of Mount Fuji, which this sheet belongs to.

With so few shapes, every decision of the carver and the printer shows. Nothing can hide in a crowd. Three places on the sheet tell you how it was made, and who made it.

**[Guided tour]** A summit, a forest, a label.

- *The last snow*: The white streaks running down from the top are not white ink. They are the paper itself, left bare. The carver cut around them, so the red block never touched these lines.
- *A forest of dots*: Hundreds of tiny dark marks stand for trees. They are thick at the bottom and thin out as they climb, then stop. Without a single trunk drawn, you understand how high the mountain is.
- *Title and signature*: The framed box gives the name of the series and of this view. The loose writing to its right is the signature. Around both, faint vertical streaks in the blue: the grain of the wooden block.

**Did you know?** Hokusai was about seventy when he designed this series, and he changed his artist name more than thirty times in his life. The signature here says, roughly, “Hokusai, now calling himself Iitsu.” He claimed that nothing he had drawn before seventy was worth much.

Because each sheet was inked by hand, no two are identical. Some early ones are pale, with a soft pink mountain and less blue. Later printings, like this one, pushed the red and the blue much harder, and that stronger version became the famous one. Collectors still argue about which is closer to what Hokusai wanted.

The series was so popular that the publisher added ten more views, making forty-six. Most of them show travelers, fishermen or carpenters with Fuji small in the distance. Here the people are gone and the mountain is alone with the weather. Go back to the red now. Is it a color, or a time of day?

## Portrait of Adele Bloch-Bauer I

*Gustav Klimt, 1907 · Neue Galerie, New York · secession*

> For decades a museum label hid her name. A niece in Los Angeles got it back.

In the 1940s this painting hung in a Vienna museum under a new label: “Lady in Gold”. No name. The woman it shows, Adele Bloch-Bauer, was Jewish, and the Nazis who had taken the picture from her family's home preferred that nobody be reminded of it.

Gustav Klimt had died in 1918, Adele in 1925. Her husband Ferdinand, a sugar manufacturer, fled Austria in 1938 when Nazi Germany annexed the country, and lost his factory, his house and his art. After the war the portrait stayed in Vienna's Belvedere museum for sixty years. Printed on posters and souvenirs, it became a kind of Austrian Mona Lisa.

**Did you know?** In 1998 Adele's niece Maria Altmann, who had escaped Vienna as a young bride and run a dress shop in Los Angeles, asked for the painting back. Her case against Austria went as far as the U.S. Supreme Court. An Austrian panel finally ruled in her favor in 2006. She was eighty-nine.

Months later the cosmetics heir Ronald Lauder bought the portrait for the Neue Galerie, his museum in New York, for a reported 135 million dollars. No painting was known to have sold for more at the time. So what is it about this square of gold? Begin with the few parts that are not gold at all.

**[Guided tour]** Find the woman inside the pattern.

- *The face*: Heavy eyelids, flushed cheeks, parted lips under a cloud of dark hair. Klimt paints her in oils with soft, lifelike shading, like a photograph set into a jewel.
- *The hands*: Her hands fold over each other at an awkward angle. Adele is said to have had a crooked finger that she liked to hide. Klimt keeps the nervous gesture instead of tidying it.
- *The choker*: A wide collar of jewels, tight around the throat. It was a real necklace. Years later it was given to Maria Altmann as a wedding present, and in 1938 the Nazis took it too.

Face, shoulders, hands: that is all the skin there is, a small island in the upper right. Everything else is flattened into ornament. You cannot tell where the dress stops and the armchair begins, or how far away the wall is. A living person seems to be dissolving into a precious object.

The shimmer is real metal. Klimt laid gold leaf and silver leaf, sheets of metal beaten thinner than paper, onto the canvas, then painted over them and pressed patterns into them. He had grown up around the craft: his father was a gold engraver.

**Did you know?** In 1903 Klimt traveled to Ravenna, in Italy, and stood before church mosaics some 1,400 years old, where a Byzantine empress and her court glitter against walls of gold. He wrote home that their splendor was beyond belief. Adele's portrait was ordered that same year.

It took him four years and more than a hundred preparatory drawings. Finished in 1907, the portrait marks the height of what is called his Golden Phase. Klimt had led the Vienna Secession, a group of artists who walked out of the city's official art society in 1897 to bring painting, design and craft together. Here a portrait turns into goldsmith's work.

The patterns are not random filling. Klimt borrowed signs from ancient Egypt, Greece and Byzantium, and gave each zone of the picture its own motif. That is the only way to tell dress, chair and wall apart. Go hunting.

**[Free zoom]** 

- *Eyes*: Down the middle of the dress, wide-open eyes set between triangles, a motif taken from ancient Egyptian art. The gown stares back at you.
- *Spirals and discs*: Coils and coin-like discs crowd around her head and shoulders like a halo. This zone is the back of the armchair she sits in.
- *Red squares*: The strongest reds in the picture: a block of squares nested inside squares, hard-edged against all the curls nearby.
- *A grey square*: A square of silvery grey set into the gold of the wall. Small touches like this keep the huge empty left side from going dead.
- *The green floor*: A strip of plain green under a checkered band. It is the single clue that this golden space is a room, with a floor to stand on.
- *The signature*: GUSTAV KLIMT in block capitals stacked into a square, with the date split in two below. He designed his name like a stamp.

Did you feel how the eye never settles? It hops from sign to sign and keeps coming back to the face, the one calm and readable thing. That pull between a human being and a dazzling surface is the whole drama of the picture.

**Did you know?** Adele is the only person Klimt painted in two full-length portraits. The second one, from 1912, has no gold at all: she stands in a broad black hat against patches of bright color.

Who was she? Born in 1881, the daughter of a banker, married at eighteen. She hosted a salon, a regular gathering at her home where writers, composers and politicians came to talk. Relatives remembered her as frail, sharp and restless, hungry for the education that women of her world were denied. She died of meningitis at forty-three.

**Key takeaway.** Klimt painted a face like a painter and everything around it like a jeweler. The tension between the two is what makes the portrait impossible to forget.

For years a label erased her name. Today the title gives it in full, and the story of the theft is told wherever the painting is shown. The gold used to be the first thing people saw. Now, perhaps, it is the woman.

## Black Square

*Kazimir Malevich, 1915 · Tretyakov Gallery, Moscow · abstraction*

> “I could have done that.” Maybe. But there are two hidden paintings under this square.

A black square on a white ground. No subject, no depth, nothing a ruler and a pot of paint could not supply. People have been saying “I could have done that” in front of this painting for more than a century. Fair enough. So try a better question: why had nobody done it before 1915?

Kazimir Malevich was 36, living in Russia in the middle of the First World War, and he had already worked his way through every modern style: Impressionism, Cubism, Futurism. Each still showed things, however broken up: a guitar, a face, a man sharpening knives. He wanted to know what would be left if the things were taken out completely.

He named the answer Suprematism: painting in which pure feeling is “supreme”, more important than any object shown. The Black Square was its starting point. Malevich called it the “zero of form”, the spot where the old art of imitating the world stops and something else can begin.

**Did you know?** At its first showing, in Petrograd in December 1915, Malevich hung the square high up across a corner of the room, tilted toward the visitors. Every Russian understood the gesture: that corner is where an Orthodox home keeps its holy icon.

So it was never meant as a prank or as decoration. It was closer to a new kind of icon, an object to stand in front of. And the real object, about 80 centimeters (31 inches) on each side, holds a surprise for anyone who knows it from books: it is not the flat, perfect, printed square you carry in your head.

**[Guided tour]** Three places where the idea turns back into paint.

- *The cracks*: The black has split into a web of cracks, like dried mud, and paler colors glint through the gaps. Malevich painted over layers that were not fully dry, so the surface pulled apart as it aged.
- *Ghost shapes*: Some zones are smooth, others crackled, with straight borders between them: a slanting band, an upright strip. They look like the outlines of shapes lying underneath the black.
- *The edge*: No ruler here. The border between black and white wavers slightly, drawn by hand, and the white is brushed on in visible strokes. Even the square is not perfectly square.

Those ghosts are real. In 2015 researchers at the Tretyakov Gallery in Moscow X-rayed the picture and found not one but two earlier compositions under the square, both full of colored shapes. Malevich did not start from nothing. He buried his own previous work, literally, under the black.

**Did you know?** The same study found a handwritten note in the white margin. Researchers read it as a nod to a French prank of the 1890s: an all-black picture by the humorist Alphonse Allais, captioned as a battle in a cave at night. Who wrote the note, and why, is still argued over.

After 1915 the square followed him everywhere. He painted at least three more versions and signed some late works with a tiny black square instead of his name. When he died in 1935, a black square was fixed to the front of the truck that carried his coffin through Leningrad.

By then Stalin's government had declared abstract art useless to the people, and the Black Square spent decades in storage, rarely shown in the Soviet Union until the 1980s. Abroad, its descendants multiplied: the single-color canvases of the 1950s, Minimalism in the 1960s, and a good share of modern graphic design.

**Key takeaway.** The Black Square matters less for how it looks than for what it allowed: after it, a painting no longer had to be a picture of anything.

So, is it art? You could not have painted it in 1915, because the idea was the hard part, and the idea was his. But Malevich wanted more than your agreement. He wanted you to stand in front of a black surface and feel something without being shown anything. Does that happen to you? Only the real, cracked object can settle it.

## Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe

*Édouard Manet, 1863 · Musée d'Orsay, Paris · realism*

> In 1863, Parisians lined up in front of this picnic to laugh at it.

Paris, spring 1863. In a side gallery, visitors press around a large canvas, and they are laughing. People nudge each other and call their friends over. Within days the newspapers are full of jokes about the picture, and of outrage too.

The gallery had a nickname: the Salon des Refusés, the “show of the rejected”. That year the jury of the official Salon, the state exhibition that could make or break a career, turned away so many works that Emperor Napoleon III let the losers hang next door. Édouard Manet, 31, was one of them.

Nudity was not the problem. The official Salon was full of bare skin that same year, and nobody blinked, because those bodies belonged to Venus or to nymphs in some faraway myth. Manet changed three or four things. They were enough.

**[Guided tour]** Four details that turned a picnic into a scandal.

- *Her eyes*: She turns her head and looks straight at you, chin on her hand, calm and a little amused. Painted goddesses looked away. She has noticed you staring.
- *Her clothes*: A blue dress, a straw hat with a ribbon, lying in a heap beside the spilled basket. A goddess is naked by nature. This woman got undressed.
- *A necktie*: The man beside her wears a dark jacket, white collar and tie: the outfit of a Parisian in 1863. No toga, no legend to hide behind.
- *The bather*: A second woman wades in her shift. She seems far away, yet she is nearly as big as the people in front, as if cut out and pasted in.

The woman in front had a name. Victorine Meurent, a model of about 18 with red hair, posed for Manet in his studio, not in a forest. The two men are thought to be one of his brothers and his future brother-in-law. They chat. Nobody looks at anybody.

**Did you know?** Victorine Meurent became a painter in her own right. In 1876 the Salon jury accepted her work in the same year it refused Manet's.

Here is the part the laughing crowd missed. The trio is copied, pose for pose, from a print after Raphael, the most respected painter of the Renaissance, showing river gods sitting on a bank. And clothed men with naked women in a landscape hung a short walk away in the Louvre, in a famous Venetian picture.

**Key takeaway.** Manet did not attack the old masters. He replayed one of their scenes with people from his own time, and the result showed how much the “noble nude” depended on costume and distance.

The handling shocked painters as much as the subject shocked the public. Academic teaching asked for modeling, the slow shading that makes a body look round. Manet lights Victorine like a flash: pale skin, almost no half-tones, set flat against dark coats. The woods behind are brushed in like a theater backdrop.

**Did you know?** The canvas measures about 2.08 by 2.65 meters (roughly 7 by 9 feet). That size was normally kept for battles, saints and kings. Manet's first title for it was simply “Le Bain”, the bath.

In all that loose green, Manet slipped in one small living thing. It has nothing to do with the picnic, and most visitors walk past without seeing it. Look high, above everyone's heads.

**[Find it]** Something is flying in this painting. Can you spot it?

- *A bird in mid-air*: A small bird with an orange breast, usually called a bullfinch, hangs at the very top with wings spread. Three or four strokes of the brush, no more.

Compare that quick bird with the cherries, the bread and the silver flask at the bottom left, painted with real care. Manet finishes what interests him and sketches the rest. To critics of 1863 this looked lazy. To younger painters it looked like permission.

Two years later a 24-year-old named Claude Monet began his own, even larger “Déjeuner sur l'herbe”, this time painted with real outdoor light. And the idea of showing outside the official Salon stuck: in 1874, Monet and his friends held the first independent exhibition of the group soon called the Impressionists.

**Did you know?** Almost a century on, Pablo Picasso became obsessed with this picture. Between 1959 and 1962 he made 27 paintings and well over a hundred drawings reworking its four figures.

The crowd of 1863 is gone, but the painting still does the same thing to whoever stands before it. Three people ignore one another, and the fourth looks out at the one person who was not invited. What does she expect you to say?

## Blue Horse I

*Franz Marc, 1911 · Lenbachhaus, Munich · expressionism*

> Franz Marc decided that blue was the color of the spirit. Then he found it a body.

In December 1910, Franz Marc wrote to a painter friend to explain what colors mean. Not what they look like: what they mean. Blue, he said, is the male principle, severe and spiritual. Yellow is the female principle, gentle, cheerful and sensual. Red is matter, brutal and heavy, the color the other two must fight and overcome.

You do not have to agree. Marc himself treated it as a private working rule, not a law. But a few months later he painted this canvas, a little over a meter tall (about 44 inches), and the rule is all over it. Test it yourself.

**[Find it]** Marc called one color heavy, earthbound matter. Tap the largest patch of it.

- *Red, underfoot*: The biggest red lies at the very bottom, under the hooves. Smaller patches sit low on the left. Red is literally the ground: the heavy stuff the horse stands on and rises above.

Now read the rest the same way. Behind the animal, a wide band of yellow, warm and soft, like a meadow in sun. And rising out of red and yellow, the horse: blue from hoof to mane, the color Marc kept for the mind and the spirit. The picture is a ladder of three colors with the animal at the top.

**Key takeaway.** Marc is an Expressionist: he chooses color for what it makes you feel and understand, not for what things look like. A horse can be blue if blue tells the truth about it.

Why give the spiritual color to a horse and not to a person? Marc had studied theology before art, and he had come to find people disappointing. Animals seemed to him purer, more at home in the world. He knew them well: he spent days sketching in pastures and even taught animal anatomy to other artists.

That knowledge shows. However strange the color, the body is right: the weight on the legs, the turn of the neck. And the pose is not the proud, prancing horse of old statues. Something quieter is going on.

**[Guided tour]** Three places where the horse stops being a zoology lesson.

- *A lowered head*: The head tilts down and to the side. No eye is drawn, only a dark hollow. He is not looking at us or at the view. He seems to be thinking, which is an odd thing to say about a horse.
- *A chest like cut stone*: The chest is built from flat, pale planes with sharp edges, almost white. Marc had seen the new Cubist painting from Paris, which broke bodies into facets. He borrows the trick to make the body glow.
- *Long, thin legs*: The legs are slender and a little unsure, one of them angled back. Many viewers see a young animal, not yet grown. It makes the horse feel like someone at the start of life.

So the horse is treated like a person with an inner life. What about the land around him? Marc did not want a backdrop. He wanted animal and world to be made of the same stuff, moving to the same beat.

**[Composition lines]** Watch one curve repeat across the canvas.

- *The mane*: The dark blue mane arches over the head and falls down the neck in one sweep.
- *The blue hill*: Right behind it, a hill takes up the same arc, in the same blue. For a second you cannot tell where the horse ends and the mountain begins.
- *The pink hill*: Lower down, a rose-colored slope swells in the same way, like a second back. The landscape is built from horse shapes.

In that same year, 1911, Marc and the Russian painter Wassily Kandinsky founded a group in Munich. They organized exhibitions and published an almanac, a book mixing essays and images, where children's drawings and folk art sat next to modern painting. Their shared idea: art should show the inner world, not the surface.

**Did you know?** The group was called Der Blaue Reiter, The Blue Rider. Kandinsky later explained the name with a shrug: they both loved blue, Marc liked horses and he liked riders. It was settled over coffee.

It lasted three years. When the First World War began in 1914, Marc was called up. He kept drawing in a pocket sketchbook at the front. In March 1916 he was killed by a shell splinter near Verdun, in France. He was 36.

**Did you know?** In 1937 the Nazi government seized Marc's paintings from German museums as “degenerate” art. His large canvas The Tower of Blue Horses was even put on show to be mocked, until veterans protested that Marc had died for Germany. It vanished at the end of the war and has never been found.

- Where is the largest patch of red in the painting? → On the ground, under the hooves
- Why did Marc prefer to paint animals? → They seemed purer to him than people
- How does the landscape relate to the horse? → The hills repeat the curves of the horse
- Who founded the Blue Rider group with Marc? → Wassily Kandinsky

**True or false**

- Marc painted the horse blue because he had never studied real horses. → False
- Franz Marc died as a soldier in the First World War. → True
- In Marc's color system, yellow stood for brutal, heavy matter. → False

**To verify**

- Color theory paraphrased from Marc's letter to August Macke of December 1910 (blue male, severe, spiritual; yellow female, gentle, cheerful, sensual; red matter, brutal and heavy). Not quoted word for word.
- Size about 112 x 84.5 cm.
- Kandinsky's later account of the name Blue Rider (both loved blue, Marc horses, Kandinsky riders).

## Woman with a Hat

*Henri Matisse, 1905 · San Francisco Museum of Modern Art · fauvism*

> A green nose, a blue hat, and one word from a critic that named a whole movement.

“Donatello among the wild beasts!” The critic Louis Vauxcelles said it in Paris in October 1905, then printed it in his newspaper. He was standing in a room of blazing paintings, in the middle of which someone had placed a polite, old-fashioned statue. Donatello was a gentle sculptor of the 1400s. The wild beasts, “les fauves” in French, were the painters.

The picture that drew the most laughter in that room was this one. Visitors pointed at it. Some reportedly tried to scratch the paint. It shows Amélie Matisse, the painter's wife, dressed up to go out: big hat, gloves, a fan. Nothing shocking in the subject. The outrage was entirely about color.

Henri Matisse was 35 and not a young rebel. He had trained for years, copied old paintings in the Louvre, and had a family to feed. He had just spent the summer of 1905 in Collioure, a fishing port in the south of France, painting side by side with his friend André Derain under a hard Mediterranean light. Something came loose there.

**Did you know?** Matisse came to art late and by accident. He was a law clerk of about twenty, stuck in bed after an operation for appendicitis, when his mother brought him a box of paints to pass the time. He later said that from the moment he held it, he knew this was his life.

Back in Paris, he painted Amélie quickly, just before the exhibition opened. Painters had always used color to copy what they saw: pink for skin, brown for shadow. Matisse cut that link. Search her face for the place where the break is most brutal.

**[Find it]** On a normal portrait, the shadow down the side of a nose is a soft brown. What did Matisse put there instead? Tap it.

- *A green nose*: A stripe of pure green runs down the nose, with more green on the forehead and jaw. Next to the pink and yellow of the cheek, it still reads as shadow. The color is wrong and the face works.

Why does it work? Because green and pink-red are opposites, what painters call complementary colors: placed side by side, each makes the other look stronger. Matisse builds the head with these clashes, cool against warm, where earlier painters used dark against light. He draws with color.

**Key takeaway.** Fauvism frees color from its job of describing things. A color is chosen for what it does next to its neighbors and for the feeling it carries, not because the object “is” that color.

**Did you know?** People tell that Amélie actually posed in black. When someone asked Matisse what color her dress had really been, he is said to have answered: “Black, of course.” True or not, the story says it well: every color here was invented.

The hat deserves its place in the title. Amélie knew hats: for several years she had run a hat shop in Paris, and its income kept the family going while nobody bought Henri's pictures. So this towering pile of feathers and flowers is also a quiet tribute to the person who paid the rent.

**[Free zoom]** 

- *The hat*: A heap of blue, violet, orange and green strokes. You cannot name a single flower or feather, yet you understand at once: an enormous, fashionable hat.
- *An orange neck*: Her neck is a band of flat orange and red. It works like a hinge of heat between the cool face and the cool dress.
- *The fan*: White, pink and lilac dabs, laid on fast with a loaded brush. The fan is suggested, never outlined. Her gloved hand below it is a few green and violet patches.
- *No room, no wall*: Behind her there is no place at all, only fields of pink, green and violet. The background is treated exactly like the figure: as color.
- *The signature*: Matisse signs in the same green he used on her face. Around it the paint is thin and scrubbed, and the pale canvas shows through in places.
- *Her eyes*: Dark, steady, a little tired, under strong arched brows. In the middle of the storm of paint, her look is calm and perfectly readable.

You may have noticed how unfinished it all looks. That was the second scandal. In 1905 a picture shown in public was supposed to be worked up and smooth. Matisse stops as soon as the colors balance, leaving strokes open and edges ragged. For many visitors this was simply a sketch that someone had dared to frame.

Matisse was badly hurt by the mockery. Then a telegram arrived: someone wanted to buy the painting. The buyers were Leo and Gertrude Stein, an American brother and sister living in Paris. They paid 500 francs, hung it in their apartment, and invited everyone they knew to come and argue about it. One of the guests was a young Spaniard named Pablo Picasso.

**Did you know?** The painting followed the Stein family to California, which is why this scandal of the Paris art world now lives in San Francisco. It was later bought by the collector Elise Haas, who left it to the San Francisco Museum of Modern Art.

**Key takeaway.** Vauxcelles meant “wild beasts” as an insult. The painters kept it as their name. Within a few years, strong color chosen freely had stopped being a scandal and become one of the basic tools of modern art.

## Ophelia

*John Everett Millais, 1852 · Tate Britain, London · preraphaelite*

> She posed in a bathtub all winter. The riverbank had been waiting since summer.

London, the winter of 1852. A young woman lies fully dressed in a bathtub of water, hour after hour, playing dead. Oil lamps under the tub are supposed to keep the water warm. One day they go out. The painter, lost in his work, does not notice. She says nothing and stays in the cold.

Her name was Elizabeth Siddal, and she was twenty-two. She came down with a severe cold. Her father threatened the painter, John Everett Millais, with legal action until he agreed to pay the doctor's bills. By then Millais had what he wanted: the face and hands of a drowning girl.

The girl is Ophelia, from Shakespeare's “Hamlet”. Hamlet, the man she loves, has killed her father, and grief has broken her mind. Hanging garlands on a willow, she falls into a stream. Her dress spreads and holds her up for a while. She sings, unaware of any danger, until the soaked cloth pulls her under. On stage you never see it happen. A queen only tells it.

Millais built the picture backward: scenery first, person last. In the summer of 1851 he sat on the bank of the Hogsmill, a small river in Surrey, south of London, for up to eleven hours a day, six days a week, about five months. He copied one stretch of bank leaf by leaf, leaving a blank in the middle of the canvas.

**Did you know?** He wrote to a friend that the flies of Surrey were more muscular than any he had met, that the wind threatened to blow him into the water, and that he had been warned he could be summoned before a judge for trespassing in a field and flattening the hay.

Such stubbornness was a creed. Millais, himself only twenty-two, belonged to the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood, a small band of young British painters who rejected the soft, brownish, flattering manner taught in art schools. Their rule: go outdoors and paint each thing exactly as it is. Even the things almost nobody will notice.

**[Find it]** A small bird is watching the scene from the branches. Where?

- *The robin*: A robin, red breast turned toward you, perched among the twigs on the far left. In her madness Ophelia sings a line about “bonny sweet Robin”.

You had to comb through a lot of twigs to get there, and every one is as sharp as the next. Nothing blurs with distance, which is not how eyes work, and it gives the scene its slightly feverish intensity. Millais also painted over a bright white ground, a white layer under the colors, so the greens glow like stained glass.

The plants are not only accurate. Victorians enjoyed a “language of flowers” in which each species carried a message, and Shakespeare names several in the play. Millais added some of his own. The stream can be read almost like a letter.

**[Guided tour]** Start with the brightest spot of color in the water.

- *The poppy*: A red poppy with a dark heart, floating near her hand. Shakespeare does not mention one. Millais slipped it in: the poppy stands for sleep, and for death.
- *Violets*: A chain of violets circles her neck. In the play Ophelia says the violets all withered when her father died. They spoke of faithfulness, and of dying young.
- *Pansies*: Purple petals drift on the skirt. Ophelia hands out pansies earlier in the play: “that's for thoughts”. They could also mean love in vain.
- *Dog roses*: Pale wild roses climb over the bank. Ophelia's brother calls her “rose of May”. A wild rose opens and drops within days: youth and beauty, briefly.

**Did you know?** There was once an animal in the water too. Millais painted a water vole paddling beside Ophelia. When relatives saw the unfinished canvas, they guessed in turn that it was a hare, a rabbit, a dog and a cat. He scraped it out.

Shown at London's Royal Academy in 1852, the picture puzzled some critics, who thought a girl in a weedy ditch lacked dignity. The public never agreed. Siddal went on to paint and write poetry herself, married the painter Dante Gabriel Rossetti, and died at thirty-two from an overdose of laudanum, an opium-based medicine. It is hard to see her floating here without thinking of that.

**Key takeaway.** The Pre-Raphaelites painted poetry with a botanist's eye: here every leaf is true to nature, and every flower means something.

You now know the picture was made in two halves, months apart: a summer riverbank and a winter bathtub. Wander through it on your own and see whether you can feel the seam.

**[Free zoom]** 

- *The face*: Lips parted, eyes half open: she is still singing. This is Siddal, painted in the London studio months after the landscape was finished.
- *Open hands*: Palms up, fingers loose. The gesture echoes saints and martyrs in older religious pictures, and lends a quiet holiness to her death.
- *The dress*: An antique gown “all flowered over in silver embroidery”, as Millais described it. He bought it secondhand for four pounds. Air caught in the cloth keeps her afloat, for now.
- *The willow*: The tree she was climbing when a branch gave way. A willow leaning over water was the emblem of forsaken love.
- *Long purples*: Tall spikes of purple loosestrife. They are probably Millais's answer to the “long purples” that Shakespeare's queen lists among Ophelia's garlands.
- *Forget-me-nots*: Tiny pale blue flowers on the far bank. Their name is the message.
- *The reeds*: Stiff green blades painted one at a time on the bank in Surrey. Compare their hard edges with the softness of her skin, added indoors.

## Udnie

*Francis Picabia, 1913 · Centre Pompidou, Paris · dada_surrealism*

> A dancer on an ocean liner, a first sight of New York, and not one recognizable shape.

January 1913. Francis Picabia, a painter from Paris in his early thirties, walks off an ocean liner in New York and is thrilled. Towers of steel, electric signs, trains running above the streets, crowds in a hurry. He tells reporters this is the one truly modern city, the place where the new art belongs. He feels he has stepped inside the future.

He had come for the Armory Show, a giant exhibition that introduced European modern art to a startled American public. Picabia was one of the very few European artists in it who made the trip, so the newspapers treated him as spokesman for the whole adventure. He was rich, funny, mad about fast cars, and he enjoyed every minute.

Something else had caught his eye on the crossing: a dancer, Stacia Napierkowska, rehearsing on the ship. Back in Paris a few months later, he poured both memories, the dancer and the city, into a canvas as tall as a room: about 2.9 by 3 meters (roughly 9.5 by 10 feet). Then he lettered an invented word on it.

**[Find it]** Picabia wrote the title on the painting itself. Where?

- *UDNIE*: In capitals along the top edge. Nobody is sure what it means. One guess: a scrambled nod to Jean d'Udine, a writer who compared painting to music. Others hear “Ondine”, a water spirit.

A word with no clear meaning for a picture with no clear subject: the match is deliberate. Search for the dancer and you will find no face, no arm, no leg. Picabia said he no longer painted what his eyes saw but what stayed in his mind afterward. This is abstraction, art that does not depict recognizable things, and in 1913 it was barely a few years old.

**Did you know?** Napierkowska's American tour went badly. Her dancing was judged indecent and she was arrested in New York. Picabia gave his painting a subtitle that keeps her in view: “Young American Girl; The Dance”.

How do you paint a dance without a dancer? With what a dance is made of: swing, rhythm, spin, sudden stops. Shapes fold over one another like cloth in motion, and a few long strokes throw your eye around the square.

**[Composition lines]** Four movements to follow.

- *A sweep*: A black line whips across the top like a flung arm or a ribbon. It launches the motion from left to right.
- *A beat*: Straight pink bars slice down the left side, stiff as drumsticks. Against so many curves they land like a hard beat.
- *A slide*: A pale pink band glides toward the bottom edge and seems to drag the whole cluster with it, like a foot sliding across a floor.
- *A core*: The brightest whites gather in the middle, curled like shells. Around them the shapes darken and the grey planes fan out to the corners, as if the picture burst from its heart.

The colors are few: greys, white and black, then patches of green, blue and flesh pink. The greys feel like steel and stone. The pinks are the only warmth, the last trace of a body. A poet friend, Guillaume Apollinaire, had coined a name for painting built from color and rhythm the way music is built from notes: Orphism, after Orpheus, the musician of Greek myth.

**Key takeaway.** Udnie does not show you a dancer. It tries to put you in the state of someone watching her.

Paris was not ready. When Picabia hung the canvas at the Salon d'Automne of 1913, the big fall exhibition, most critics were baffled or hostile. He shrugged. Within two years he was back in New York, already tired of pure abstraction and drawing spark plugs and cameras as portraits of his friends. He was on his way to Dada, the movement that used nonsense as a weapon.

**Did you know?** Picabia changed styles the way he changed cars, and he is said to have owned more than a hundred of those. One of his sayings: “If you want to have clean ideas, change them as often as your shirts.”

## The Night Watch

*Rembrandt, 1642 · Rijksmuseum, Amsterdam · siecledor*

> The world's most famous night scene takes place in broad daylight.

Two things everyone knows about this painting are wrong. It does not happen at night, and nobody in it is on watch. The nickname appeared about a century and a half after Rembrandt painted it, when layers of old varnish had turned the whole surface the color of strong tea.

What Rembrandt delivered in 1642 was a group portrait of an Amsterdam militia, a company of well-off citizens who trained with weapons to defend their city. Their captain, Frans Banninck Cocq, is the man in black. He is giving the order to march. It is daytime.

**Key takeaway.** The darkness here is a painter's choice, not the hour. Rembrandt sends a beam of sun into deep shade so that he can decide exactly what you see first.

Militia portraits were a Dutch specialty, and usually a dull one. Everybody paid a share, so everybody expected to be seen: neat rows of faces, like a class photo. Rembrandt took the same commission and painted the minute before the parade, when no one is yet in place.

The company's weapon was the musket, an early long gun that took many steps to fire. Rembrandt hides a small lesson in how to use one, spread over three men, reading from left to right.

**[Guided tour]** Follow the musket across the canvas.

- *Loading*: The man dressed entirely in red tips powder into the barrel of his musket. He looks down, absorbed in the job.
- *Firing*: Half hidden behind the captain, a small figure with oak leaves on his helmet lets off a shot in the middle of the crowd. You only see the helmet and the gun.
- *Cleaning*: Just behind the lieutenant, an older man in a helmet bends over his gun and blows the leftover powder out of its pan.

Those three poses come straight from the drill books of the time. In a manual they are stiff diagrams. Here they become noise: a shot goes off, a drum rolls on the right, pikes clatter overhead. No earlier group portrait had tried to be loud.

**Did you know?** The company paid Rembrandt 1,600 guilders, several years of wages for a skilled worker. Each man chipped in about 100, a bit more or less depending on how well he would be shown. Some of them ended up as half a face behind an elbow.

The two leaders got their money's worth. The captain's left hand seems to poke out of the picture toward you. That trick is called foreshortening: drawing something shorter than it is so that it appears to come forward. Beside him walks his lieutenant, dressed in pale yellow.

**[Find it]** The captain's outstretched hand casts a shadow. Where does it fall?

- *On the yellow coat*: The dark shape of fingers lies across the lieutenant's embroidered coat, just below his sash. The shadow ties the two officers together.

That one shadow tells you where the sun is: high on the left, in front of the men. It also shows Rembrandt's favorite tool, chiaroscuro, an Italian word for strong contrast between light and dark. Black suit against yellow suit, lit face against a wall of shade.

At 3.63 by 4.37 meters (about 12 by 14 feet), the canvas is bigger than most living room walls, and the figures in front are life-size. To keep such a mass of people from turning into a traffic jam, Rembrandt organizes it with a few long poles.

**[Composition lines]** Flag, lance and weapons work like arrows.

- *The flag*: On the left, the flag bearer lifts the company's banner. Its pole leans outward and up, opening the group toward the top corner.
- *The lance*: On the right, a long lance tilts the other way. Together the two poles spread like an opening fan, with the officers at its hinge.
- *The lieutenant's weapon*: The lieutenant's short ceremonial spear points down and forward, out of the picture. It aims at the spot where you are standing.
- *The step*: From the captain's open hand down to his leading foot: the whole company is about to walk forward, off the canvas and into the room.

A fan opening, a step forward. This sense of a scene caught mid-movement, spilling toward the viewer, is what historians call Baroque, the theatrical style of the 1600s. In the militia's hall, the painting hung beside six calmer group portraits by other artists. It must have looked like a door thrown open.

**Did you know?** In 1715 the painting moved to Amsterdam's Town Hall, where it did not fit the wall. So it was cut down on all four sides. The left edge lost the most, and two militiamen went with it. We know them only from a small copy made before the trim.

Not everyone here is a paying member. Rembrandt added extras to fill out the crowd: children, a drummer hired for the day, and the strangest figure of all, a girl in a gold dress who glows as if she carried her own lamp.

**[Guided tour]** Four details that no militiaman paid for.

- *The golden girl*: She has a child's body and an oddly grown-up face. She is no portrait of a real person: she works as a living emblem of the company, a kind of mascot.
- *The chicken*: A dead white bird hangs from her belt, claws up. A claw was the symbol of this company, known as the Kloveniers, so she wears their badge.
- *The shield*: Barely visible on the dark arch, an oval shield lists the names of 18 men who paid. It was added some years later, probably at their request.
- *The dog*: A thinly painted dog crouches and barks at the drummer. You can see the floor through its body: Rembrandt sketched it with a few light strokes.

Eighteen names on the shield, thirty-four figures on the canvas. The gap is Rembrandt's own cast: sixteen people nobody ordered. He treated a business portrait the way a director treats a stage, and that freedom is what later painters admired most.

A stubborn legend says the militiamen hated the result and that it ruined him. Nothing in the records supports this. The captain even had copies made for himself. Rembrandt's hard years had other causes: his wife Saskia died in 1642, and his spending led to bankruptcy in 1656.

**Did you know?** During the Second World War the canvas was taken off its frame, rolled around a cylinder and moved from one hiding place to another. It spent the last years of the war in caves near Maastricht.

It has also been attacked with a knife in 1975 and sprayed with acid in 1990, and repaired both times. Since 2019 the Rijksmuseum has been studying and restoring it inside a glass room, in full view of visitors. In 2021, a computer trained on Rembrandt's style even printed the strips cut off in 1715.

So keep the nickname, everyone does. But the next time you see it, picture the varnish gone and the shutters open: a company of Amsterdam shopkeepers and merchants, stepping out of the shade into the afternoon sun.

## Bal du moulin de la Galette

*Pierre-Auguste Renoir, 1876 · Musée d'Orsay, Paris · impressionnisme*

> A Sunday dance in Montmartre, painted so that the sunlight never holds still.

A polka, slightly too fast. Shoes scraping on packed earth. A chair dragged back, a glass set down, forty conversations at once and somebody laughing over all of them. This painting makes no sound, of course. Yet most people who stand in front of it say they can nearly hear it.

The place is the Moulin de la Galette, an open-air dance garden on the hill of Montmartre, which in 1876 was still half village on the edge of Paris. On Sunday afternoons, working people climbed up there: seamstresses, shop clerks, florists, apprentices. They put on their best clothes and danced until evening.

**Did you know?** “Moulin” means mill: a real windmill stood on the site. The family who owned it sold visitors a galette, a plain flat cake, with something to drink. The cake gave the dance hall its name.

Renoir was 35 and short of money. He rented a studio a few streets away and recruited his crowd from two groups: girls from the neighborhood, who agreed to pose, and his own friends, painters and writers, who had no choice. One of those friends is easy to pick out, thanks to a habit.

**[Find it]** Someone in this crowd is smoking a long, thin pipe. Can you spot him?

- *The pipe smoker*: Top hat, short beard, a white clay pipe between his fingers. He sits at the table on the right with two companions, half listening, eyes somewhere else.

The three men at that table belonged to Renoir's circle, two painters and a young writer, Georges Rivière. Rivière later described those weeks in a book, which is why we know so much about who posed. The man in the straw hat beside the smoker seems to hold a pencil: he may be the one taking notes.

Notice what Renoir refuses to do. An older painter would have built this scene around a hero: one couple, one face, with everyone else arranged around them. Here nobody is in charge. Your eye lands on a face, slides to a hat, jumps to a dress. That is exactly how you look around at a party.

**[Free zoom]** 

- *The dancers*: A woman in pink turns her face to us while her tall partner bends toward her. Tradition names them Margot, a favorite model, and a Cuban painter friend. They dance in the only clear patch of ground.
- *The girl on the bench*: Striped dress, pink and blue, and a dreamy half smile aimed at someone outside the picture. She is said to be Estelle, the sister of one of Renoir's models.
- *A hand on her shoulder*: A second woman in black leans over the girl and rests a hand on her. She listens to the men at the table. The two heads form the calm center of the storm.
- *The glasses*: A bottle and tall glasses, a few fast strokes each. The reddish drink is usually said to be grenadine, a sweet syrup with water. An afternoon out did not have to be expensive.
- *Mother and child*: In the lower left corner, a woman bends toward a small blond girl. Families came too. Renoir wanted the dance to look respectable and gentle, not wild.
- *A small face*: Easy to miss: a child's round face looks out between the adults, at about the height of their elbows.
- *The gas lamps*: Clusters of glass globes hang above the crowd. They are not lit yet. When evening comes, the dance will go on under gaslight.

**Did you know?** The canvas measures about 131 by 175 centimeters (roughly 4 by 6 feet). According to Rivière, friends carried it from the studio to the garden and back on working days, and one windy afternoon it nearly flew away like a kite.

Why go to such trouble instead of painting indoors from sketches? Because the true subject was only available on site. The garden was planted with trees, and the afternoon sun came through the leaves in pieces. Renoir wanted those pieces, on every surface they touched.

**[Guided tour]** Follow the light that falls through the leaves.

- *A spotted jacket*: The man with his back to us wears a dark jacket, covered in pale round patches. They are not a pattern in the cloth. They are coins of sunlight that slipped between the leaves.
- *A straw hat*: Where the sun hits it directly, the hat turns nearly white, with a ribbon of deep blue. The face under the brim stays in cool shade.
- *The ground*: The beaten earth should be brown. Renoir paints it in pink, lilac and sky blue, with loose strokes that never settle. The floor itself seems to be dancing.

Something else is at work in those patches. The shadows are not black or brown, as painters had been taught to make them. They are blue and violet. Renoir and his friends had noticed that outdoors, shade picks up the color of the sky. Even the men's dark suits here are built from deep blues.

**Key takeaway.** Impressionism in one idea: paint what the eye receives at a given moment, light and color first, and let the outlines take care of themselves.

Renoir showed the picture in 1877, at the third exhibition organized by the group of painters now called the Impressionists. Reactions were split. Some writers loved its cheerfulness. Others could not get past the blotches: to them, the light on the clothes looked like stains, and the faces looked unfinished.

**Did you know?** The first owner was another painter. Gustave Caillebotte, who had family money, bought works from his struggling friends and left his collection to the French state when he died. That gift is why this canvas now belongs to a Paris museum.

Renoir also made a smaller version of the same scene. In 1990 it sold at auction for 78.1 million dollars, at the time one of the highest prices ever paid for a painting. A long way from grenadine and flat cake.

Montmartre is a district of Paris now, and the dance garden is long gone. The band packed up well over a century ago. On this canvas it is still about four o'clock on a Sunday, the sun is still coming through the leaves, and the polka has not finished.

## The Burghers of Calais

*Auguste Rodin, 1889 · Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York · symbolism*

> Calais ordered one hero on a pedestal. Rodin delivered six frightened men at street level.

Calais, northern France, August 1347. The English army has surrounded the town for eleven months and the food is gone. King Edward III, furious at how long it has taken, makes an offer: he will spare the people if six of the leading citizens, the burghers, walk out to him ready to die.

His conditions are precise. They must come bareheaded and barefoot, in their shirts, with ropes around their necks, carrying the keys to the town and its castle. According to the chronicler Jean Froissart, the richest man in Calais, Eustache de Saint-Pierre, stood up first. Five others followed.

Five centuries later, in 1884, Calais decided to honor them with a monument and turned to Auguste Rodin, 43, a sculptor with a rising and controversial name. The town council pictured one hero, Eustache, high on a pedestal. Rodin read Froissart and came back with all six.

He chose the worst minute of the story. The men have gathered and are about to start walking toward the English camp. None of them knows he will survive. Each is alone with the decision he has just made, and one of them carries what the king demanded.

**[Find it]** Which of these men holds the key to the city?

- *The key*: Jean d'Aire grips an enormous key in both fists, low in front of his body. His jaw is set, his back straight. Of all of them, he is the one who has decided not to flinch.

Now compare him with his companions, because Rodin gives each one a different answer to the same fear. That was the scandal. Monuments were supposed to show courage. This one shows doubt, grief and exhaustion right beside it, and ranks none above the others.

**Did you know?** Rodin first modeled every one of the men naked, to get the weight and tension of each body right. Only afterwards did he hang the heavy shirts on them. Several of those nude studies survive as sculptures in their own right.

Travel across the group from left to right. Pay as much attention to the hands as to the faces: Rodin made them larger than life, heavy as tools, so that they would speak from a distance.

**[Guided tour]** The photo shows one side of the group. Some of the men are half hidden behind the others.

- *The raised hand*: The man on the left turns his head down and away and lifts an open hand, as if starting a sentence that will not come out. His body is already leaving.
- *The oldest*: Bearded, head sunk forward: this is usually identified as Eustache de Saint-Pierre, the first volunteer. No heroic pose at all. He looks very tired.
- *Head in hands*: At the right edge a man bends over and clutches his skull. A thick knotted rope hangs beside him. This is plain despair, cast in bronze for a public square.
- *An open palm*: A hand hangs open at hip height, fingers spread, bigger than a real one. Rodin often let a hand carry as much feeling as a face.

Four temperaments, yet the same coarse shirt and the same patch of ground. When the Calais committee saw the model, it complained that the men looked defeated and that the group lacked the usual elegant outline. The usual outline was a pyramid: lesser figures below, the hero rising at the top, one clear front. Rodin threw that out, and you can trace how.

**[Composition lines]** How the group is built.

- *No summit*: The heads reach almost the same height. Nobody rises above the rest, so your eye cannot pick out a leader.
- *A block*: The group fits inside a box, not a pyramid. It has no front or back: you are meant to walk around it and meet each man in turn.
- *The gap*: Empty air separates the man on the left from the others. They share one fate, but they do not touch or look at each other.

A block of equals, and Rodin wanted it low: set almost on the paving stones of the square, so that the people of Calais would brush past the burghers and feel the walk as their own. The city refused. At the unveiling in 1895 the bronze stood on a tall stone base.

**Did you know?** Rodin got his way only after his death in 1917. In 1924 Calais finally lowered the monument to near ground level in front of its town hall, where it still stands.

The end of the story, by the way, is kinder than the sculpture. Froissart tells that the English queen, Philippa of Hainault, who was pregnant, begged her husband for mercy, and the six were set free. Rodin knew it. He left the rescue out.

**Did you know?** The bronze in this photo is one of several originals. French law allows twelve casts of a Rodin sculpture, and all twelve Burghers now exist. The first is in Calais. This one, at the Metropolitan Museum in New York, was cast in 1985. Others stand in London, Paris, Tokyo and Seoul.

**Key takeaway.** Rodin replaced the hero on a pedestal with ordinary people at ground level, each afraid in his own way. After the Burghers, a public monument could be about what courage costs.

## Bathers at Asnières

*Georges Seurat, 1884 · National Gallery, London · neo_impressionism*

> Turned down by the jury, hung beside a snack bar, now one of London's most loved paintings.

In the spring of 1884, the jury of the Paris Salon answered a 24-year-old painter with one word: refused. The Salon was the giant official exhibition where careers were made. Georges Seurat had spent about a year on a single canvas for it, and the jury would not hang it.

What he had sent was enormous: 2 meters high and 3 meters wide (about 6.5 by 10 feet). That size was traditionally kept for kings, battles and gods. Seurat used it for a few working men and boys from the edge of Paris, cooling off by the river on a hot day.

**Did you know?** Seurat showed the painting anyway, weeks later, at a new exhibition organized by rejected artists, with no jury at all. It was so big that it was reportedly hung in the refreshment room, next to the bar.

The place is Asnières, on the Seine northwest of Paris. On a riverbank in a painting you might expect a pretty countryside. Seurat tells you, far in the distance, exactly what kind of neighborhood this is.

**[Find it]** Something on the horizon explains where these bathers probably spend their working week. Can you spot it?

- *Factory chimneys*: A row of chimneys at Clichy, one sending dark smoke into the summer haze, behind a railway bridge. This is an industrial suburb, and the smoke is painted as tenderly as the sky.

So these are not gentlemen on holiday. The bowler hat, the heavy boots and the plain clothes dropped on the grass belong to workers and shop boys on their time off. Yet nobody splashes or laughs. Each figure sits inside his own silence, solid as a statue.

That stillness was built indoors. Seurat went to the riverbank again and again, making about fourteen small oil sketches on wooden panels and around ten careful drawings of models. Then, in his studio, he assembled the big picture piece by piece, like an architect.

**[Composition lines]** Here is the scaffolding he built his summer afternoon on.

- *The bank*: The edge of the grass cuts the canvas corner to corner. Land and resting bodies fill one side, water and light the other.
- *The far shore*: A calm horizontal of bridge, factories and trees steadies the slanting bank, the way a horizon steadies a boat.
- *Steps into the river*: Three heads go down like stairs: the boy sitting on the bank, the boy waist-deep, the boy in the red hat. Your eye wades into the water with them.

One more thing holds the picture together: nearly everyone faces the same way, to the right, toward the opposite bank. Seurat shows almost every figure in strict side view, like the carved processions on ancient Greek and Egyptian walls that he had studied as a student.

**Key takeaway.** Seurat gave ordinary workers the scale and the stillness that art had reserved for heroes. The subject is modern; the calm is ancient.

He was just as methodical about color. Seurat read scientists who had shown that a color changes according to its neighbor: a pale tone looks paler beside a dark one, and orange looks more intense beside blue. He decided to paint those effects on purpose, with different brushwork for each kind of surface.

**[Guided tour]** Three places where you can see him testing his ideas about color.

- *A halo of water*: Beside the boy's shaded face and chest, the river turns paler than it is further out. Seurat exaggerates the contrast so the figure stands out from the water without a drawn outline.
- *Crisscross grass*: The lawn is woven from short strokes going in several directions, greens mixed with yellow and touches of pink. From a distance they blend into sunlit grass.
- *The red hat*: The hat glows against the blue river because orange-red and blue are opposites. Its surface is speckled with small dots of other colors, unlike the smoother skin beside it.

Those dots on the hat are a later signature. Around 1887 Seurat went back to this canvas and retouched a few areas with the tiny points of pure color that became his famous method, often called pointillism. Bathers is the picture painted just before he invented it.

And the people across the water, the ones the bathers seem to be gazing toward? Seurat painted them next. The opposite shore is the island of La Grande Jatte, where the better-off Parisians strolled on Sundays. His second giant canvas shows that side, entirely in dots.

**Did you know?** No one bought Bathers at Asnières during Seurat's lifetime. It stayed with him until he died suddenly in 1891, at only 31, leaving just a handful of large paintings.

Listen to the picture for a second. The only sound in it comes from the boy in the red hat, hands cupped around his mouth, calling out across the river. We never learn to whom, or whether anyone on the other side calls back.

**Did you know?** Forty years after the Salon refused it, the painting was bought for the British nation in 1924 with money given by the textile manufacturer Samuel Courtauld. It hangs in the National Gallery in London.

## Terracotta Army

*Unknown (China), -210 · Mausoleum of the First Qin Emperor, Xi'an · world*

> Farmers digging a well in 1974 hit a clay head. Eight thousand soldiers were standing behind it.

March 1974, in the countryside near the city of Xi'an, in central China. It had not rained for months. A few farmers chose a spot in a persimmon orchard and began digging a well. A few meters down, a shovel hit something hard. It was not a rock. It was a head made of baked clay, with a face.

They found a torso next, then bronze arrowheads. Villagers had turned up bits of pottery in these fields before and thought little of it. This time the news reached archaeologists, who arrived expecting a short job. They have now been digging for half a century, and they are not finished.

What you see in this photo is called Pit 1. It is about 230 meters long and 62 wide, more than two soccer fields end to end, under a roof built like an aircraft hangar. Before meeting the soldiers, get a sense of the ground plan, because the army was not simply buried in a hole.

**[Composition lines]** The pit is a piece of architecture. Trace its bones.

- *A wall of earth*: This thick ridge is a wall of pounded earth. It runs the full length of the pit, straight as a ruler.
- *Its neighbor*: A second wall, parallel to the first. Between the two lies a sunken corridor, paved with brick, where the soldiers stand four abreast. Pit 1 has eleven such corridors.
- *And another*: Seen from here, the walls rush toward one point far away, like railway tracks. That is how long the pit is. The army faces you, and it faces east.

**Did you know?** The farmers were paid a small reward for the pieces they handed in. Years later, one of them was given a seat in the museum shop, where he signed guidebooks for tourists as “the man who discovered the Terracotta Army.”

The man they guard lies about a mile to the west, under a wooded hill. He became king of the state of Qin at thirteen. By 221 BC he had conquered every rival kingdom and taken a new title: Qin Shi Huang, First Emperor. He standardized writing, money, weights, even the width of cart axles.

**Key takeaway.** The army is one part of a giant tomb complex for China's First Emperor, who died in 210 BC. It was made to serve him in the afterlife, and it was never meant to be seen by the living.

Work on the tomb began almost as soon as he took the throne and went on for more than thirty years. An ancient historian speaks of 700,000 laborers. The soldiers were produced like nothing before them: legs, torsos, arms, hands and heads made separately, joined, then fired. Each figure stands about 1.8 to 1.9 meters tall (around 6 feet) and is hollow from the waist up.

**[Guided tour]** Come down to the front of the pit.

- *The front ranks*: Robes belted at the waist, hair tied in a knot on top of the head. After assembly, workers reshaped each face by hand: a different mustache, different cheekbones. That is why no two look alike.
- *Four horses*: At the bottom edge, four clay horses stand side by side. They once pulled a wooden war chariot. The wood rotted away long ago and left the team waiting for nothing.
- *Ripples in the wall*: The top of the earth wall is ridged like a washboard. Heavy timbers once lay across here, carrying a roof of mats and soil. Their weight left this print in the earth.

So the soldiers once stood in dark, roofed tunnels. Then the roof came down. Charred beams show that the pits burned, perhaps when rebel troops broke in to grab the weapons a few years after the emperor died. Tons of earth dropped onto the ranks.

**Did you know?** The gray-brown color is an accident. Every figure was painted: pink faces, black hair, robes in red, green, purple and blue. The paint sat on a layer of lacquer that curls and flakes off within minutes once the damp soil around it dries. Much of the army is left underground until this can be prevented.

Almost every warrior you see standing has been rebuilt from fragments, sometimes a hundred or more per figure. The photo shows the whole process at once: finished ranks, work in progress, earth never opened, and the people of today looking down at it.

**[Free zoom]** 

- *Still in pieces*: Farther back, the neat rows dissolve. Bodies lie tipped over and broken, as the falling roof left them. This is how the whole army looked when it was uncovered.
- *The living*: A dark line along the railing: visitors. They give you the scale. The clay men are a little taller than most of the people looking at them.
- *Not yet dug*: Toward the back, the corridors end in flat banks of bare earth. About 6,000 figures are thought to be in this pit. Only around 2,000 have been brought out.
- *More horses*: On the right, another team of horses, noses toward the wall. Horses were made at close to life size, with tails and manes neatly trimmed.
- *A number*: A small numbered sign. The pit is still an active excavation, mapped out section by section, and also one of the most visited sites in China.
- *The hangar*: The steel roof was put up in the 1970s, right over the dig. Daylight comes through a long strip of glass. Nothing was moved: the museum was built around the army.

The hands of the soldiers are curled around empty air. They once held real weapons, not clay copies: bronze swords, spears, crossbows with working triggers. Many were looted in ancient times, but tens of thousands of arrowheads remained in the soil.

**Did you know?** Dozens of figures carry a name stamped or scratched into the clay, in a hidden spot. About 87 different names have been found. They belong to the foremen in charge. If a soldier cracked in the kiln, the officials knew whom to punish.

Pit 1 is only the infantry. Two smaller pits nearby hold archers, cavalry, chariots and what looks like a command post. Elsewhere around the tomb, diggers have found clay acrobats, stone armor and bronze water birds. The emperor took a whole world with him, not just a guard.

And the emperor? His burial chamber has never been opened. The same ancient historian wrote that it holds a map of the empire with rivers of flowing mercury, a liquid metal. It sounded like a fable until scientists tested the hill and found unusually high levels of mercury in the soil.

Chinese archaeologists have decided to wait. Having watched the paint peel off the soldiers within minutes, they will not open the tomb until they are sure they can protect what is inside. Would you have that patience, knowing what a single well turned up?

## Las Meninas

*Diego Velázquez, 1656 · Prado, Madrid · baroque*

> Nine people, one dog, and a painter who seems to be painting you.

Count the people in this room who are staring straight at you. The painter is. The little girl in white is. So is the woman in the dark dress on the right. Now the uncomfortable question: why would a Spanish court of 1656 be looking at you?

The room is real. It was a long gallery in the Alcázar, the royal palace in Madrid, where Diego Velázquez had his studio. He was 57, painter to King Philip IV for more than thirty years. And he has put himself in the picture, brush in hand, behind a canvas so tall we only see its back.

**[Find it]** Two people in this painting are not standing in the room with the others. Tap where you think they are.

- *The mirror*: On the back wall, one frame glows while the paintings around it stay dark. It is a mirror. In it, under a red curtain, are the faint heads of King Philip IV and Queen Mariana.

If the king and queen appear in a mirror facing us, they must be standing where we stand. That solves the puzzle, and opens three more. Is Velázquez painting their portrait? Does the mirror reflect them, or the canvas he is working on? Have they just walked in to interrupt? The painting never says.

**Key takeaway.** Las Meninas puts the most important people outside the picture, in your place. You are not looking at a scene. You are standing in it.

**Did you know?** For almost two centuries the picture had no poetic name. Palace inventories called it “The Family of Philip IV.” The title Las Meninas only appeared in a Prado catalog of 1843. A menina was a young noblewoman who served a royal child.

The girl at the center is the Infanta Margarita, five years old, the king's daughter. Everyone around her has a job at court, and Velázquez knew each of them by name. What makes the group feel alive is that no two of them are doing the same thing with their eyes.

**[Guided tour]** Follow the glances across the room, from the painter to the far door.

- *The painter*: Velázquez leans back from his canvas, brush paused. It is the look of someone comparing a model with what he has painted so far. The model is wherever you are.
- *The infanta*: Her head turns to the left but her eyes slide back toward us, as children do when something more interesting enters a room.
- *Maribárbola*: A court dwarf from Germany, in dark blue. She faces us squarely and without ceremony, the steadiest gaze in the whole picture.
- *The man on the stairs*: In the bright doorway, José Nieto, the queen's chamberlain, stops on the steps and looks back. He is the only one who can see everything: the room, the canvas and the royal couple.

Some have noticed, some have not yet. The maid kneeling on the left is still busy with the infanta. The boy on the far right is teasing the dog with his foot. That mix is why the scene feels like one second caught in mid-air, two hundred years before the first camera.

There is ambition behind the charm. In Spain, painting was still taxed and treated as a manual trade, like carpentry. Velázquez wanted it recognized as a noble art, and himself as a nobleman. So he shows the painter thinking rather than working, at home among royalty, as tall as anyone in the room.

**Did you know?** The red cross on Velázquez's chest is the badge of the Order of Santiago, a knighthood he received only in 1659, three years after the painting was finished. It was added later. A legend says the king painted it on himself.

Now forget the people and consider how the canvas is divided. For a group portrait, it is built in a strange way: a great deal of it contains nobody at all.

**[Composition lines]** Three lines show how the room is organized.

- *The empty half*: Everything above this line is ceiling, shadow and dim paintings. All eleven figures live in the lower half. The dark space above them is what makes the room feel so high and so quiet.
- *A wave of heads*: Join the heads from the painter to the boy and you get a line that dips, rises and dips again. Nobody is lined up as in an official portrait.
- *Light from the right*: Daylight enters from a window on the right that we cannot see. It lands hardest on the infanta's pale hair and dress, so she shines without needing to be the largest figure.

A second light comes from the open door at the back, far behind everyone. Between those two lights, Velázquez paints something very hard to paint: the air itself. Figures get softer and grayer the deeper they stand, exactly as they would in a long, dusty room.

He does it with astonishingly little. From across the gallery in Madrid the silks look sewn and starched. Walk up to the canvas, which is about 3.2 meters tall (over 10 feet), and the dresses turn into loose flicks and smears of paint. The detail is made in your eye, not on the surface.

**[Free zoom]** 

- *The red cross*: The Cross of Santiago, painted onto the black doublet some years after the rest.
- *A cup of water*: The kneeling menina, María Agustina Sarmiento, offers the infanta a small red clay jug on a tray. Scented clay cups like this were a fashionable way to drink cool water.
- *The hidden canvas*: We see only the wooden stretcher and the raw back of the cloth. It is roughly as tall as Las Meninas itself, which has made many people wonder whether this is the picture he is painting.
- *The mastiff*: A big, sleepy Spanish mastiff, patient under the boy's foot. Its fur is brushed in with a few broad, dry strokes.
- *Nicolasito*: Nicolasito Pertusato, a young Italian dwarf at court, nudges the dog. His is the only real movement in the room.
- *Two in the shadows*: A chaperone in a widow's white headdress speaks to a guard who is barely there, his face left as a blur. Velázquez lets the half-light swallow them.
- *The second menina*: Isabel de Velasco begins a curtsy. Her body bends toward the infanta, but her eyes have already gone to the visitors.

**Did you know?** On Christmas Eve 1734 the Alcázar burned down. Hundreds of paintings were lost. Las Meninas was rescued, damaged and later trimmed at the edges. The palace it shows no longer exists: the Royal Palace of Madrid stands on its site.

Painters have been circling this canvas ever since. The Italian Luca Giordano, seeing it a generation later, called it “the theology of painting,” the highest thing the art could do. In 1957 Picasso shut himself away for months and painted 58 variations on it, pulling it apart figure by figure.

Velázquez died in 1660, four years after finishing it, and left no explanation. So the first question still stands. When you face Las Meninas, are you the king, the queen, a visitor at the door, or simply the next person the painter has decided to study?

## The Last Supper

*Leonardo da Vinci, 1498 · Santa Maria delle Grazie, Milan · renaissance*

> One sentence has just been spoken. Leonardo paints the second after.

“One of you will betray me.” The sentence has just left Jesus's mouth. Nobody has had time to answer. What Leonardo paints is the second that follows: twelve friends at dinner, hit by the same words at the same moment.

Earlier painters showed this meal as a quiet row of saints. Leonardo wanted a room in uproar. He studied how real people take bad news and listed gestures in his notebooks: a man turning with a knife still in his hand, another showing his open palms, another bending to whisper in his neighbor's ear.

**[Guided tour]** The twelve apostles split into four groups of three. Follow the news as it travels along the table.

- *The far left*: At the end of the table, Bartholomew has jumped to his feet and leans on both hands, straining to hear. Two seats along, Andrew lifts both palms: not me.
- *Beside Jesus*: Thomas pushes a raised finger forward. James throws his arms wide and pulls back. Behind them Philip presses both hands to his chest, as if asking: surely you don't mean me?
- *The far right*: Too far away to be sure of what they heard, the last three turn to each other. The man in blue points both hands back toward Jesus while his face questions his neighbors.

In the middle of this storm, one figure does not move. Jesus sits with his arms open and his eyes lowered. And one group was missing from that tour: the three men seated just to the left of him as you face the wall. The traitor is among them.

Painters before Leonardo made Judas easy to spot. They sat him alone on the near side of the table, with his back to the viewer. Leonardo puts him in the row with everyone else. To catch him, you have to read bodies.

**[Find it]** Which of these men is Judas? Tap him.

- *Judas*: He recoils, and his face drops into shadow: the only dark face at the table. His elbow rests on the cloth and his right hand closes around a small purse, the price of the betrayal.

Behind him, grey-haired Peter lunges toward young John to whisper something, and pushes Judas forward as he does. Guilt, anger and sorrow are packed into a single knot of three heads. Nobody in the room has noticed the guilty man yet. You have.

**Did you know?** The prior of the convent complained that the painter was taking forever. According to the writer Vasari, Leonardo replied that he was still searching for a face wicked enough for Judas, and that if pressed, he could always use the prior's.

This picture is not on canvas. It covers the end wall of a dining hall, the refectory of the convent of Santa Maria delle Grazie in Milan, and it is enormous: about 4.6 by 8.8 meters (15 by 29 feet). The Duke of Milan, Ludovico Sforza, ordered it around 1495. The friars ate below it, as if Jesus's table were one more table in their own room.

To make the wall seem to open onto a second room, Leonardo used perspective: a way of drawing in which parallel edges shrink toward a single point, so a flat surface looks deep. Where he placed that point is no accident.

**[Composition lines]** Trace the edges of the painted room and see where they lead.

- *Left wall*: The tops of the dark wall hangings step down toward the back of the room. Extend that slope and it keeps going, straight to one spot.
- *Right wall*: The same happens on this side. Both lines meet at Jesus's head. Every edge of the architecture points at him.
- *A still triangle*: His head and open arms draw a triangle, a shape that sits firmly on its base. Around him bodies twist and lean. He alone is at rest.

**Did you know?** Restorers found a small hole in the plaster at Jesus's temple. Leonardo seems to have driven a nail there and stretched strings from it to rule the lines of his perspective.

**Key takeaway.** Leonardo turned a holy scene into human drama: one sentence, thirteen reactions, and a room in which every line leads to the calm man at the center.

Now the bad news. Wall paintings were normally done in fresco: color brushed onto wet plaster, which locks it in as it dries. Fresco demands speed. Leonardo liked to hesitate and retouch. A boy who lived at the convent later recalled him painting from dawn to dusk without eating, then staying away for days. So he worked on a dry wall, with a mix of tempera and oil of his own devising.

The experiment failed. The paint never bonded with the damp wall and began to flake within about twenty years, while Leonardo was still alive. By the middle of the 1500s visitors described a blur of stains. Much of what you see is what survived, patiently cleaned by restorers between 1978 and 1999.

People were not always gentle with it either. Wander over the wall and you will find one brutal scar, along with a few details that made it through five centuries.

**[Free zoom]** 

- *The door*: In 1652 the friars enlarged a doorway in this wall. It sliced through the bottom of the painting and took Jesus's feet with it. They are lost for good.
- *Three windows*: Openings onto blue hills. The middle one sits right behind Jesus's head and frames it with daylight: a natural halo instead of a golden disc.
- *Thomas's finger*: A raised index finger. Thomas is the apostle who, in the Gospel, will later ask to touch Jesus's wounds before he believes.
- *Peter's knife*: A hand bent backward at the hip holds a knife. It is Peter's. Later that night, in the garden, he will draw a blade to defend Jesus.
- *Bread and wine*: Jesus's hands open toward the bread and the wine on the table. This supper is also the origin of Communion, the Christian rite of sharing both.
- *The tablecloth*: Blue woven bands and crisp fold lines, as if the linen had just been taken from a chest. Leonardo painted the cloth of an ordinary, well-kept table.

The worst came in August 1943, when an Allied bomb struck the convent. The roof of the refectory and one of its long walls collapsed. Leonardo's wall, shielded by sandbags and scaffolding, was left standing among the rubble, open to the sky.

**Did you know?** The refectory is now a sealed, climate-controlled room. Visitors enter in small groups and may stay fifteen minutes. That is about how long the scene itself would last before someone finally answered.

## Vitruvian Man

*Leonardo da Vinci, 1490 · Gallerie dell'Accademia, Venice · renaissance*

> A Roman architect set a riddle about the body. Fifteen centuries later, one sheet of paper solved it.

Around 25 BC, a Roman architect named Vitruvius wrote down a claim about the human body. Lay a well-built man on his back, he said, with arms and legs spread, and a circle drawn from his navel will touch his fingers and toes. Stand him up with arms out, and he fits a square just as well, because his arm span equals his height.

The passage sits in a book about building temples. Vitruvius's point was that good architecture should copy the proportions of the body. The book had no surviving pictures, so when Renaissance scholars rediscovered it, artists started trying to draw the man in the circle and the square. Most attempts looked stretched or stiff.

Leonardo da Vinci took up the riddle around 1490, while working in Milan. Others had assumed the circle and the square must share the same center, like a target. Check whether Leonardo agrees.

**[Composition lines]** Four lines reveal Leonardo's solution.

- *The circle*: With arms raised and legs apart, fingertips and soles touch the circle. Its center is the navel, exactly as the Roman text demands.
- *The square*: Feet together and arms level, the man touches all four sides. The square rests on the bottom of the circle but pokes out of it at the upper corners.
- *Span equals height*: From fingertip to fingertip, the outstretched arms are as wide as the man is tall. That is why the standing figure fits a square at all.
- *Two centers*: The circle turns on the navel. The square's center lies lower, at the groin. Leonardo lets the two shapes slide apart, and the body suddenly fits both without being distorted.

That small shift is the whole discovery. Leonardo did not bend the man to obey the geometry, as earlier artists had. He trusted the body and moved the geometry. Then he went further and quietly corrected the ancient author where his own measurements of real men disagreed.

**Key takeaway.** Vitruvian Man is an experiment as much as a drawing: an old text tested against a real body, with the body winning.

**Did you know?** The sheet is small, about 34 by 24 centimeters (13.5 by 9.5 inches), hardly bigger than a page of printer paper. Leonardo first scratched in the construction with a metal point and compass, then went over it in pen and brown ink.

There is also a trick of time in it. You see one man with four arms and four legs, and you read him without effort as a single body changing position. Combine the limbs in your head and he jumps between poses, a little like the frames of an animation, four hundred years early.

**Did you know?** The lines of text run backward, from right to left, and can be read normally in a mirror. Leonardo was left-handed and wrote his private notes this way all his life, perhaps simply to avoid smudging wet ink with his hand.

So this is a page from a working notebook, with words wrapped around the figure. The writing lists measurements, and the drawing carries small marks most people never notice because they stop at the famous silhouette.

**[Free zoom]** 

- *Notes above*: In mirror writing, Leonardo begins by naming Vitruvius, then lists the units: four fingers make a palm, four palms a foot, six palms a cubit, four cubits a man.
- *The face*: A stern, frowning face framed by thick curls, far more individual than a diagram needs. Faint lines cross it, marking the proportions of chin, nose and brow.
- *The navel*: The compass point stood here. Every part of the circle is the same distance from this spot.
- *The ruler*: Under the square runs a measuring line divided by small ticks into palms, with finer finger marks at both ends. It is the drawing's own scale bar.
- *A foot in profile*: One standing foot points at us, the other turns sideways. Shown from the side, its full length can be measured: Leonardo makes a foot one seventh of the height.
- *A raised hand*: The middle finger of the raised arm reaches the circle at the same level as the top of the head, a relation Leonardo spells out in his notes.
- *Notes below*: More proportions in mirror writing: the arm span equals the height, the face is a tenth of it, and so on down the body, each one a simple fraction.

Why did such fractions matter so much? Thinkers of Leonardo's time loved the idea that man is a small model of the universe. The circle stood for the heavens, the square for the earth. A body that touches both was, for them, proof that humans belong to both worlds.

Leonardo never published the sheet. It stayed among his papers, passed through private collections, and entered the Accademia in Venice in 1822. Only later did it become a universal logo for medicine, science, sport and “the human being” in general.

**Did you know?** Italians carry this drawing in their pockets: it is stamped on the Italian one-euro coin. The original, in contrast, is almost never on view. Light fades the ink, so it lives in a dark, climate-controlled store and is shown only briefly every few years.

When France asked to borrow it in 2019 for the 500th anniversary of Leonardo's death, an Italian heritage group went to court to stop the loan, arguing the sheet was too fragile to travel. The judges let it go. It hung in the Louvre for a few weeks, then went back into the dark.

Later artists and architects kept returning to the question Leonardo answered here. In the twentieth century the architect Le Corbusier designed a whole system of building measurements around a standing man with a raised arm. The idea that our rooms should be cut to our bodies starts with Vitruvius, but it has this face.

- Why does the handwriting look strange? → It is mirror writing, running from right to left

## Winged Victory of Samothrace

*Unknown (Greece), -190 · Louvre, Paris · antiquity*

> No head, no arms, no feet. And still she looks like she is about to take off.

Start with the inventory of what is gone. The head. Both arms. Both feet. One whole wing, which the museum had to rebuild in plaster. Whatever she held, whatever her face expressed, the paint that once colored her. By any normal count, this is a wreck.

And yet she is one of the three or four most visited objects in the Louvre. People climb a long staircase toward her and stop talking. So the useful question is not what is missing. It is what is left that can do this to a crowd.

She is Nike, the Greek goddess of victory, always shown with wings because victory arrives fast and can fly off again. She was carved around 190 BC, in a period called Hellenistic, when Greek sculptors loved drama, motion and strong feeling. Under her feet is the gray stone front of a warship. She has just landed on its deck.

**[Composition lines]** The whole monument is built from three directions that pull against each other.

- *The ship*: The prow, the pointed front of the ship, drives toward the left. That is the direction of travel, straight into the wind.
- *The body*: She leans forward from the ankles, chest ahead of her hips, like someone bracing on a moving deck. Her weight has not settled yet.
- *The wing*: The wing sweeps up and back, still open to slow her down. Body forward, wing back: this tug in two directions is what reads as movement.

**Key takeaway.** The sculptor did not carve a goddess standing on a boat. He carved one instant: the touch-down, when the wings are still working and the cloth has not yet fallen still.

**Did you know?** She was found in April 1863 on Samothrace, a small Greek island, by Charles Champoiseau, a French diplomat digging in an ancient sanctuary. She came out of the ground in many pieces. The big gray blocks nearby were left behind: nobody understood they were a ship until twelve years later.

Now the wind, since you cannot carve air. The sculptor makes it visible through what it does to cloth. Her dress is a thin tunic, belted under the chest, with a heavier cloak that has slipped down around her hips. The stone is marble from the island of Paros, prized because it can be cut very fine.

**[Guided tour]** Three places where stone behaves like wet fabric in a gale.

- *Cloth like water*: Over the stomach the tunic is pressed so flat by the wind that the navel shows through it. The marble here is carved into a skin of thin ripples.
- *Deep folds*: Lower down, the cloak twists between her legs in thick ropes with dark hollows cut far into the block. Thin cloth above, heavy cloth below: the contrast makes both convincing.
- *A sail of stone*: Behind her hip, a fold of the cloak balloons backward, caught by the air. It is solid marble, hanging in space.

This trick has a name: wet drapery, cloth carved as if soaked so that it clings to the body and reveals it. Greek sculptors had used it for two centuries. Here it is pushed to an extreme, and it gives the figure a second job. You read her body and the weather at the same time.

In ancient Samothrace she stood outdoors, high on a hillside above the sanctuary, probably set up to thank the gods for a win at sea. Nobody knows which battle. She was meant to be seen at an angle from her left side, close to the angle of this photograph, and the sculptor finished the other side with less care.

**Did you know?** In 1950, almost ninety years after the statue, archaeologists on Samothrace dug up her right hand. The palm is open and the fingers hold nothing. That ended an old theory that she was blowing a trumpet: she was most likely just raising her arm in greeting. The hand now sits in a glass case near her.

The Louvre placed her at the top of its grand staircase in the 1880s, on her rebuilt ship. The whole monument stands about 5.5 meters high (18 feet), the figure alone about 2.75 meters (9 feet). The choice of spot was a piece of theater: you approach her from below, as if from the water.

**Did you know?** In September 1939, with war beginning, the Louvre emptied its galleries. The Victory, weighing several tons, was eased down the staircase on a wooden ramp, held by ropes, while the curators watched in silence. She spent the war hidden in a castle in the French countryside and came back unharmed.

One last thought about the missing parts. With a face, she would be a particular woman with a particular expression, and you would look there first. Without one, your eye has nowhere to rest except the motion itself. The damage turned a statue of a goddess into something closer to a statue of flight.

**Did you know?** Her name went on to an unexpected career. In 1971 a young American shoe company called itself Nike, after the goddess, and paid a design student 35 dollars for a logo meant to suggest a wing in motion.

Before you leave her, go over the surface on your own. Several of the things listed as missing at the start have left traces you can find: a neck, a shoulder, the spot where a foot once pressed on the deck.

- *Feathers*: This is the ancient wing. Short, scale-like feathers near the top give way to long flight feathers, each carved on its own, the way a real bird's wing is layered.
- *The lost arm*: The shoulder ends in a break. The arm on this side is gone, and the tunic gathers in fine folds around the gap.

## American Gothic

*Grant Wood, 1930 · Art Institute of Chicago · american*

> The most parodied couple in America is not a couple, and never stood in front of that house.

You have met these two before, even if you have never seen the painting. They have been redrawn as presidents, cartoon families, puppets, pets and zombies. They have sold cereal and insurance. Stand two people side by side, hand one a tool, keep a straight face, and everyone gets the reference.

The original is smaller than its fame: about 78 by 65 centimeters (31 by 26 inches), painted in 1930 on beaverboard, a cheap panel of pressed wood fiber. Its painter, Grant Wood, was a 39-year-old from Cedar Rapids, Iowa, almost unknown outside his state.

**Did you know?** It began with a house, not with people. In the small town of Eldon, Iowa, Wood noticed a plain white cottage with one fancy church-style window upstairs. He sketched it on the spot and decided to paint the kind of people he imagined should live there.

That pointed window explains the title. “Carpenter Gothic” was the name for wooden American houses that borrowed shapes from medieval Gothic cathedrals. Wood found the ambition both funny and touching. Then he built his whole picture out of the window's tall, upright lines.

**[Composition lines]** Four shapes that rhyme with each other.

- *The window*: A pointed arch split by a thin center bar: the Gothic detail that started everything.
- *The pitchfork*: Three prongs, close to dead center, gripped like a weapon. It stands between you and the house like a gate.
- *The overalls*: The seams on the bib repeat the fork: three uprights joined by a curve. Wood hid the tool in the man's own clothes.
- *The faces*: Even the heads obey. Both faces are pulled long and narrow, like the boards on the wall behind them.

Window, fork, seams, faces: everything stands at attention. The stiffness was a choice. On a trip to Munich in 1928 Wood had studied Flemish and German portraits of the 1400s, with their hard outlines, glassy detail and unsmiling sitters, and he decided to paint Iowa the same way.

The pair never stood in front of the house, or even together. The woman is Wood's sister, Nan Wood Graham, then about 30. The man is his dentist, Dr. Byron McKeeby, 62. Each posed separately. And they are not meant as husband and wife: Wood described them as a farmer and his grown daughter.

Wood dressed Nan in an old-fashioned apron and pulled her hair back tight, the way women appear in family photographs from the 1800s. Everything about her is buttoned, pinned and ironed. Almost everything.

**[Find it]** One thing about the woman has escaped her tidy control. Tap it.

- *The loose curl*: A single strand of hair has slipped free and snakes down behind her ear. It is the one disorderly thing in the entire picture, and it makes her human.

That curl hangs beside the other telling detail: the eyes. He stares straight at you through round glasses, guarding the doorstep. She looks off to the side, at something we cannot see. Two people a foot apart, in different worlds. Is she anxious, bored, disapproving? Wood leaves it hanging.

**Did you know?** Wood entered the painting in a competition at the Art Institute of Chicago in the fall of 1930. It won a bronze medal and a $300 prize, and the museum bought it for another $300. It has stayed there ever since.

Then newspapers printed it, and Iowa was not amused. Readers wrote in, furious that Wood had painted them as grim, pinched fanatics. He protested that he was an Iowan himself and meant affection, not mockery. He never fully settled the matter, and the picture works either way.

The mood flipped within a few years. As the Great Depression deepened, the same hard faces began to look like stubborn people who would get through it. A satire became a patriotic emblem, and Wood became the star of Regionalism, a group of painters who turned away from European abstraction toward scenes of the rural Midwest.

A picture that reads as mockery and as praise has to be built with great care. Wood filled it with small, exact things, each one a clue about this household. Wander around and collect them.

**[Free zoom]** 

- *The cameo*: A carved brooch with a woman's profile, the single ornament she allows herself. Wood is said to have bought it in Italy as a present for his mother.
- *Porch plants*: Potted plants on the porch, among them a spiky snake plant. Somebody waters these. It is the gentlest corner of the picture, and it is on her side.
- *The drawn curtain*: The Gothic window is covered by a patterned curtain in broad daylight. Whatever goes on in this house is private.
- *Round glasses*: Thin wire spectacles and a mouth set like a ruled line. The model was a dentist, not a farmer: this expression is what Wood asked for, not a portrait of the man's character.
- *The red barn*: A barn slips in at the right edge, the strongest warm color in the picture. That side is his territory, as the porch plants are hers.
- *The apron*: A brown print apron dotted with tiny circles and edged with rickrack, a zigzag trim. It was already old-fashioned in 1930.
- *The signature*: GRANT WOOD 1930, lettered on the denim at the bottom edge, as if stitched into the overalls.

**Did you know?** The house still stands in Eldon, Iowa. A visitor center next door lends out aprons, jackets and a pitchfork so that people can pose for the photograph themselves.

**Key takeaway.** American Gothic rests on one repeated upright shape and one unanswered question: is Wood laughing at these people or honoring them? Because it never answers, it can be reused for anything.

Not every remake is a joke. In 1942 the photographer Gordon Parks posed Ella Watson, a Black cleaning woman in a government building in Washington, in front of the American flag, a broom in one hand and a mop behind her. He called the photograph American Gothic. Same stiff pose, a very different America.

## Along the River During the Qingming Festival

*Zhang Zeduan, 1100 · Palace Museum, Beijing · east_asian*

> A whole city on a strip of silk you could roll up and carry under your arm.

You were never meant to see this painting all at once. Its first owners laid it on a table, held the roll in the left hand and opened it with the right, about an arm's length at a time. Then they rolled up what they had seen and opened the next stretch. It is a film you wind by hand.

This format is called a handscroll. Here it is a ribbon of silk about 25 centimeters high (10 inches) and more than 5 meters long (17 feet), painted in ink and light color. It shows a day in Bianjing, today's Kaifeng, capital of China's Song dynasty and around the year 1100 one of the largest cities on earth.

**Did you know?** People have counted the inhabitants of the scroll. The usual tally is more than 800 human figures, along with dozens of boats, carts, sedan chairs, donkeys, oxen and camels. Many of the people are smaller than a fingernail.

Chinese handscrolls are read from right to left, like Chinese writing of the time. So the story starts at the right-hand end, and it starts quietly. The painter makes you walk into town the way a traveler would, with the noise growing as you go.

**[Guided tour]** Four stops, from the right end of the scroll to the left, in the order a reader would unroll them.

- *Open country*: The scroll opens among misty trees and fields outside the city. There is a lot of bare silk here and very little happening: a slow first breath before the crowd.
- *The river*: The trees give way to the Bian River, the city's supply line. The long dark shapes are heavy cargo boats, the kind that carried grain to the capital, moored along the bank.
- *The bridge*: A single wooden arch leaps over the water with no pillars beneath it. People called such a bridge a rainbow bridge. This is the busiest spot in the scroll and its dramatic peak.
- *The city streets*: Past the bridge the buildings crowd together: the city gate, inns, shops and stalls packed along the main street. The empty silk of the opening has completely filled up.

At the bridge, Zhang Zeduan stages a small emergency. A big boat is drifting toward the arch with its mast still being lowered. The crew scrambles with poles, and people on the bridge lean over the rail to shout and throw ropes. After two meters of calm, the scroll suddenly has a plot.

The buildings and boats are drawn in a style called ruled-line painting: the artist used a ruler and fine brush to make beams, planks and rigging as exact as an engineer's plan. Historians of technology study this scroll to learn how Song ships were steered and how such a bridge was fitted together.

**Key takeaway.** A handscroll has no single viewpoint. The painter floats above the scene and moves along with you, so the picture unfolds in time, like a walk, not like a view through a window.

About the artist we know almost nothing. Zhang Zeduan's name comes from a note written on the scroll in 1186, decades later. By then the city he had painted was gone: Bianjing fell to invaders from the north in 1127. For later readers the scroll became a memory of a lost golden age.

**Did you know?** Nobody is sure what the title means. Qingming is a spring festival when families sweep their ancestors' graves. But the two characters also mean “bright and peaceful,” so the title may simply praise a calm, well-governed age.

A scroll this famous passed through many hands over nine centuries: emperors, officials, private collectors. In China, owners did not hide that. They signed their admiration directly onto the object.

**[Guided tour]** Go to the two ends of the scroll, where the pale mounting begins.

- *Seals at the start*: The small red marks are seals, stamps carved with a name and pressed in red paste. Each one says, in effect, “this was mine” or “I saw this.”
- *Seals at the end*: More red stamps close the scroll. Beyond this point, later admirers added written comments on extra lengths of paper, so the object kept growing after the painter was done.

The last emperor, Puyi, took the scroll with him when he left the Forbidden City in the 1920s. It vanished in the chaos of war, then turned up in 1950 in a museum storeroom in northeast China, where a curator recognized it among other works. It went home to Beijing, to the Palace Museum.

**Did you know?** At the 2010 World Expo in Shanghai, the scroll was shown as a giant animated projection, about thirty times its real size. The tiny figures walked and the boats moved, and day turned into a night lit by lanterns.

The real silk is so fragile that the museum shows it only once every several years, and visitors have queued for hours to file past it. They see it fully unrolled in a long glass case. Its first readers saw less at a time, and probably more: one arm's length, held in the hands, then rolled away.
